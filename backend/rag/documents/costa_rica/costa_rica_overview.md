# Costa Rica — Overview

Costa Rica occupies a narrow stretch of Central America between Nicaragua to the north and Panama to the south, with coastlines on both the Caribbean Sea and the Pacific Ocean. Despite covering only 51,000 square kilometres — roughly the size of West Virginia — the country contains an extraordinary biodiversity, hosting around 5% of the world's known species within its borders. Volcanic mountain chains run the length of the country, separating distinct ecological zones: dry tropical forest in the northwest, cloud forest in the central highlands, lowland rainforest on the Caribbean side, and beach-and-jungle coastline along much of the Pacific.

The country abolished its standing army in 1948 and redirected the savings into education, health care, and conservation. Roughly 25% of the national territory is protected within national parks, biological reserves, and refuges — one of the highest proportions of any country in the world. This conservation infrastructure, combined with stable democratic governance and a population that has embraced ecotourism as a national identity, has made Costa Rica one of the world's most successful nature-tourism destinations.

The climate has two seasons rather than four. The dry season, from December through April, brings reliable sunshine, low humidity by tropical standards, and is the peak tourist period — particularly the weeks around Christmas, New Year, and Easter when prices spike sharply. The green season, from May through November, sees afternoon rainfall (typically intense for one to two hours, then clear), more frequent storms in September and October, and significantly thinner crowds. The Caribbean side has a different rainfall pattern with no truly dry month; September and October, paradoxically, are often the driest there.

Best time to visit depends on the destination within the country and the activity priorities. December through April is the standard recommendation for first-time visitors targeting beaches, wildlife, and active travel, with the trade-off of higher prices and more crowded national parks. Late April, May, and November offer excellent value with mostly good weather. The Caribbean coast and Tortuguero National Park are best visited September and October when nesting sea turtles are at peak activity. Surfers should consult swell calendars by region — the Pacific north has best surf November through April, while the southern Pacific peaks May through October.

Costa Rica is moderately priced by Central American standards but more expensive than first-time visitors expect. Daily costs of $80–150 cover comfortable mid-range accommodation, three meals, internal transport, and one or two paid activities. Budget travel using public buses and basic guesthouses is possible at $40–60 per day. The country's popularity has produced a tier of luxury ecolodges, beachfront boutique hotels, and adventure resorts with prices matching mid-tier European destinations — $300–800 per night is common at this level, with the most exclusive properties exceeding $1,500.

The country divides into several distinct regions for travel planning. The Central Valley contains the capital San José and the surrounding coffee country; the Arenal region in the north-central highlands holds the famous volcano and hot springs. The Pacific Northwest (Guanacaste) features the country's most developed beach tourism. The Central Pacific coast around Manuel Antonio combines beach, jungle, and accessible wildlife. The Osa Peninsula in the south is the most biodiverse area and the most remote. The Caribbean coast around Puerto Viejo and Tortuguero offers a culturally distinct Afro-Caribbean atmosphere. The Monteverde cloud forest in the central mountains is one of the country's marquee destinations. Most first-time visits combine three or four of these regions over 8–14 days.

Distances are short on the map but driving times are long because of mountain terrain and frequently poor road conditions. San José to Manuel Antonio is 160 kilometres but takes 3 hours; San José to Monteverde is 140 kilometres but takes 3.5–4 hours due to the rough access roads. Internal flights via SANSA and Aerobús connect the major destinations and reduce day-long drives to 30–60 minute flights at $80–180 one way. Renting a 4x4 is the standard mode for independent travel; the road infrastructure has improved significantly over the past decade but rural roads remain rough.
