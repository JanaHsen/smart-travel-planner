# Costa Rica — Things to Do

Wildlife observation is the country's foundational activity and the variety is exceptional even by tropical-destination standards. Manuel Antonio National Park, on the central Pacific coast, is the most accessible introduction — a half-day guided walk reliably produces sightings of three monkey species (capuchin, howler, squirrel), two-toed and three-toed sloths, agoutis, basilisk lizards, and dozens of bird species. The park is small and busy; entering at opening time with a certified guide is the standard recommendation. Corcovado National Park on the Osa Peninsula represents the opposite extreme — a vast, remote rainforest with the highest biodiversity density in the country and far smaller visitor numbers. Multi-day guided treks through Corcovado produce sightings of tapirs, scarlet macaws, all four Costa Rican monkey species, and occasionally jaguars and pumas; these trips require advance planning and are physically demanding.

The Arenal region centres on a perfectly conical 1,633-metre volcano that was actively erupting from 1968 to 2010. While dormant now, the surrounding national park, hot springs, and adventure activities have made the area one of the country's busiest tourist hubs. Hanging bridges trails through the rainforest canopy provide an immersive view of forest ecology; Mistico Park is the most established operator. Hot springs range from public natural pools (a few thousand colones for entry) to elaborate resort spa complexes ($60–150 per day). The volcano hike itself is restricted because of the still-cooling lava fields; the standard hike is to the lower slopes for views back toward the cone.

Monteverde cloud forest is one of the world's most accessible cloud forest ecosystems and a textbook example of the high-altitude tropical biome. The Monteverde Biological Reserve and the adjacent Santa Elena Reserve offer guided walks that reveal the moss-draped trees, tank bromeliads, hummingbirds, quetzals (best seen February–May), and the strange world of cloud forest understory species. Hanging bridge trails and zip-line canopy tours both originated in Monteverde and operate from multiple locations; the original Selvatura park combines the bridges, zip lines, hummingbird gardens, and a small museum. Night tours, leaving around 5:45 p.m., produce sightings of frogs, snakes, kinkajous, olingos, and tarantulas not seen during the day.

Costa Rica's beaches divide into Pacific and Caribbean and into developed and remote categories, each with distinct characters. Tamarindo and Playa del Coco in Guanacaste are the most developed Pacific resorts, with the broadest range of accommodation, restaurants, and activity infrastructure. Santa Teresa on the Nicoya Peninsula has emerged as the country's most fashionable beach destination, with surfing, yoga, and a high-end dining scene. Manuel Antonio combines beach access with adjacent rainforest. Drake Bay on the Osa Peninsula and the Caribbean coast around Puerto Viejo remain less developed and feel further removed from mainstream tourism. Sea turtle nesting on the Caribbean side at Tortuguero (July–October peak) and on the Pacific at Ostional (Olive Ridley arribadas, weather-dependent year-round but most reliable July–November) are spectacular natural events.

Surfing is world-class and the country has dozens of breaks suitable for all experience levels. Tamarindo, Nosara, and Santa Teresa offer beginner-friendly waves with dedicated surf schools (group lessons run $40–70). Pavones in the south Pacific has one of the world's longest left-hand point breaks, working best May through October. Salsa Brava off Puerto Viejo on the Caribbean is a heavy reef break for experienced surfers, working December through March. Jacó, the most accessible major surf town from San José, has consistent waves but lacks the laid-back atmosphere of the more remote breaks.

Adventure activities cover the full menu typical of the genre. Whitewater rafting on the Pacuare River, rated Class III–IV, runs as one-day trips from San José or two-day expeditions with overnight at riverside lodges. Canyoning combines rappelling down waterfalls with river hiking and is offered from multiple bases including Arenal and the Turrialba region. Zip-line canopy tours operate at virtually every major destination; the longer and higher tours (Monteverde, Arenal, Diamante in Guanacaste) are more memorable than the shorter resort-area courses.

Coffee farm visits offer a more contemplative experience and provide context for one of the country's defining industries. Doka Estate near Alajuela is the most accessible from San José; Mi Cafecito in the Central Valley offers a similar experience with smaller groups. The Britt coffee tour at the company's main facility is a more polished, theatrical version. Higher-end farm visits in the West Valley region (Naranjo, San Ramón) offer working-farm experiences with significantly less staging.

Snorkeling and scuba diving are good but not the country's defining activities — the Caribbean coast offers passable reef snorkeling but the visibility and reef health do not match Belize or Honduras. The Pacific side has excellent diving for big-fish encounters at Coco Island, but Coco is a 36-hour boat ride from the mainland and accessible only via multi-day liveaboard expeditions ($4,000–7,000 per person). Daily Pacific diving from coastal hubs like Playa del Coco produces routine encounters with white-tip reef sharks, eagle rays, and large schools of pelagic fish.

Multi-day activities and circuits include sloth and bird-focused tours in the Caribbean lowlands, multi-day horseback expeditions through Corcovado, and the increasingly popular wellness retreats on the Nicoya Peninsula — one of the world's five "Blue Zones" of exceptional longevity. Ayahuasca and other plant-medicine retreats have proliferated in the past decade with widely varying quality and ethical standing; researching operators carefully is essential.
