# Costa Rica — Practical Information

Accommodation in Costa Rica spans a wide range. Hostel dorm beds run $15–30 per night in major destinations; private rooms in hostels and budget guesthouses cost $40–80. Mid-range hotels and small ecolodges run $80–180 per night for a double room. Boutique hotels and well-positioned ecolodges in the major nature destinations cost $200–450 per night. The high-end ecolodge and beach resort tier — properties like Lapa Rios, Pacuare Lodge, Nayara, and the Four Seasons Papagayo — runs $500–2,000 per night. Peak season (mid-December through April) sees prices roughly 40–60% above shoulder rates and requires booking 3–6 months ahead at popular properties; the weeks around Christmas, New Year, and Easter command additional premiums. The green season (May–November) offers exceptional value, particularly mid-week.

Food costs are moderate. Sodas — small family-run restaurants serving casado, the standard rice-beans-protein-salad plate — charge $5–9 per meal and represent the best food value in the country. Mid-range restaurants charge $15–30 per main course; tourist-area restaurants in Manuel Antonio, Tamarindo, and Monteverde charge $25–45. High-end restaurants at the boutique hotels and ecolodges run $60–120 per person for tasting menus. Fresh fruit, including pineapple, mangoes, papaya, and tropical fruits unfamiliar to most visitors, is exceptional and inexpensive at roadside stands and local markets. The local Imperial beer and the Cacique sugar-cane spirit are the standard local drinks; craft beer and good wine selections have grown substantially over the past decade.

Transport in Costa Rica is the most logistically challenging aspect of independent travel. Renting a car is the standard mode for visitors planning to cover multiple regions; a 4x4 is essential for many destinations including Monteverde, the Osa Peninsula, and parts of Nicoya. Daily rates run $50–120 plus mandatory insurance ($15–30 per day). The mandatory insurance situation is a frequent source of confusion — Costa Rica requires liability insurance from the rental company that cannot be waived even with credit-card coverage; budget accordingly. Roads have improved significantly but potholes, unmarked sections, river crossings (in some areas), and steep mountain grades require careful driving. Mountain roads at night are particularly hazardous due to fog, livestock, and pedestrian traffic; finishing all driving before dark is the practical rule.

Public buses are extensive, cheap, and reliable but slow — a long-distance journey can take three times the equivalent driving time. The buses serve nearly every populated area in the country at fares of $5–15 per leg. Tourist shuttle services like Interbus and Gray Line operate between major destinations at $50–80 per leg with hotel pickup and drop-off, splitting the difference between public buses and rental cars. Internal flights via SANSA and Aerobús connect San José with most major destinations including Quepos (Manuel Antonio), Tamarindo, La Fortuna (Arenal), Drake Bay, and Tortuguero; flights run $80–180 per leg and reduce 4–5 hour drives to 30–60 minute flights. Luggage limits on these small aircraft are strict (typically 14 kg).

Juan Santamaría International Airport (SJO), 20 kilometres west of San José, handles the bulk of international arrivals. Liberia International Airport (LIR) in Guanacaste serves direct flights from many North American hubs and is the more practical entry point for visitors going directly to the Pacific Northwest beaches. Direct flights from major North American cities run 2–7 hours; from Europe direct flights operate from Madrid, Frankfurt, Amsterdam, and a handful of seasonal connections. Pre-booked transfers from SJO to the major destinations cost $80–250; taxis and Uber are alternatives within the Central Valley.

Cards are widely accepted at hotels, mid-range and upscale restaurants, and most tourist-oriented shops. Smaller restaurants, public buses, and rural businesses are cash-only. Both US dollars and Costa Rican colones are accepted essentially everywhere; many tourist-area prices are quoted in dollars and many cash-back transactions return change in dollars. ATMs are widely available in cities and most beach towns; rural areas and remote ecolodges may have none for hours' drive in any direction. Credit-card foreign-transaction fees and ATM withdrawal fees vary; using cards strategically and carrying moderate cash reserves is the practical approach.

Connectivity has improved substantially. Most cafes, restaurants, hotels, and tour operators offer reliable WiFi. Tourist SIMs from Kolbi, Movistar, or Claro cost $15–25 for a multi-week package with reliable coverage in populated areas. Remote ecolodges may have WiFi only in common areas, with mobile coverage limited or absent.

Health considerations are moderate but real. Tap water is potable in most of the country including San José, Monteverde, and the Pacific Northwest; bottled water is the safer default in remote areas and on the Caribbean coast. Mosquito-borne illnesses including dengue (year-round, peaks in green season), Zika, and chikungunya are present; daytime mosquito repellent is essential in lowland areas. Yellow fever is not a risk for direct arrivals from North America or Europe but vaccination is required for visitors arriving from certain South American and African countries. Sun exposure at low latitudes is intense; serious sun protection matters even on cloudy days. Crocodile and sea snake encounters are uncommon but possible at certain river mouths and Pacific beaches; local signage and guide advice should be heeded.

Safety considerations are generally moderate. Costa Rica is among the safer Latin American destinations but petty theft, particularly from unlocked rental cars and unattended beach belongings, is common. Standard travel precautions — not leaving valuables visible in cars, not bringing valuables to beaches, locking accommodation — handle most risks. Violent crime against tourists is rare. The Caribbean coast around Limón has a slightly higher rate of street crime; the popular tourist areas (Puerto Viejo, Cahuita) are largely safe but evening caution in less-trafficked sections is sensible.

Visa requirements are straightforward — most Western nationalities receive a 90-day tourist stamp on arrival. Proof of onward travel and accommodation are occasionally requested. The country charges a $29 departure tax that is now included in most ticket prices but should be confirmed with the airline.
