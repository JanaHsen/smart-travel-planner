# Patagonia, Argentina — Practical Information

Patagonia is the most expensive region in Argentina and pricing in southern hubs like El Calafate, El Chalten, and Ushuaia rivals or exceeds mid-tier European destinations during peak season. Hostel dorm beds run $25–40 per night. Mid-range guesthouses and small hotels with private rooms cost $80–180 per night. Boutique hotels and wilderness lodges run $250–600 per night. The famous Eolo Lodge near El Calafate and similar high-end estancias can exceed $1,000 per night during peak season. Booking three to six months ahead is essential for January and February; last-minute availability often does not exist in the smaller towns.

Food costs in Patagonia are higher than in Buenos Aires and far higher than the Argentine national average. A full meal at a casual restaurant runs $15–25 per person; mid-range parrilla-style steakhouses charge $30–50 for an asado dinner with wine. The famous Patagonian lamb, slow-roasted over open flame, is the regional specialty and worth seeking out at least once. Supermarket prices for self-catering are reasonable but selection is limited in smaller towns. Argentine wine, particularly Malbec from Mendoza, is excellent and far cheaper than imported options.

Transport is the single most challenging logistical factor. The road network is minimal and distances enormous. Internal flights are essential for most itineraries: Buenos Aires to El Calafate runs $150–280 one way and takes 3 hours; Buenos Aires to Ushuaia $200–350 and 3.5 hours; Buenos Aires to Bariloche $130–250 and 2.5 hours. Aerolineas Argentinas and Flybondi are the main carriers; pricing is volatile and last-minute fares often double or triple. Long-distance buses are extensive but slow — Buenos Aires to Bariloche is 22 hours by overnight bus.

Renting a car in Patagonia provides freedom that no other transport mode matches but adds significant cost and logistical complexity. Daily rates run $60–120 for a small car in peak season; cross-border rentals into Chile require advance permission paperwork and additional insurance. Fuel is expensive and stations are sparse; tanks should be topped up at every opportunity in remote regions. The famous Ruta 40, running the length of Patagonia, is paved for most of its length but includes long unpaved sections in the south. Winter driving requires winter tires and chains; many roads close entirely in heavy snow.

The Argentine peso has experienced sustained high inflation and the official exchange rate diverges significantly from the parallel "blue dollar" rate. As of late 2024 and into 2025, tourist-facing businesses in Patagonia commonly accept US dollars directly, often at advantageous rates compared to peso conversions. Bringing crisp US $100 bills allows favourable exchange at established casas de cambio in larger towns. Credit card transactions are charged at the official rate, which is usually less favourable than cash in dollars. ATM withdrawals are limited to small amounts per transaction with substantial fees; planning around cash is more practical than depending on cards.

Connectivity in Patagonia is uneven. The major towns have reliable mobile data and WiFi at hotels, hostels, and most cafes. Remote areas including significant stretches of national parks and the road between major towns have no signal at all. Pre-loaded offline maps (Maps.me, organic maps, downloaded Google Maps regions) are essential for any driving itinerary. The Movistar and Claro networks have the broadest coverage; tourist SIMs are available in Buenos Aires and at most border crossings.

Weather preparation is the most consequential aspect of Patagonia trip planning. Layered clothing covering temperatures from -5°C to 25°C is necessary even in peak summer because conditions can change within an hour at higher elevations and on exposed terrain. Genuinely waterproof outer layers are essential, not optional — afternoon storms with horizontal rain are routine. Sturdy hiking boots, sun protection (the ozone hole produces unusually intense UV in southern Patagonia), and wind-blocking gear matter more than for most other destinations. Quality outdoor gear is available in El Calafate, El Chalten, and Bariloche but at a significant markup over Northern Hemisphere prices.

Visa and entry rules are simple for most Western nationalities — a 90-day tourist stamp on arrival, extendable once. Crossing into Chile and back is straightforward at the marked border posts but adds 2–4 hours to drive times depending on queues; bringing a printed copy of any rental car cross-border permission letter is essential. Argentina requires proof of yellow fever vaccination only for visitors arriving from specific other South American countries; routine vaccinations are sufficient for direct arrivals from North America or Europe.
