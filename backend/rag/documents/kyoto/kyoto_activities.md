# Kyoto, Japan — Things to Do

The temples and shrines of Kyoto are the obvious starting point, but the variety among them is greater than first-time visitors expect. Kinkaku-ji, the Golden Pavilion, is the most photographed and accordingly the most crowded — arrive at opening or in the last hour before close to avoid the worst of it. Ginkaku-ji, the Silver Pavilion, despite its name was never actually clad in silver and is far less crowded; its gardens of raked sand and moss are arguably more refined. Kiyomizu-dera, perched on wooden stilts over the eastern hillside, offers the best panoramic view of the city, particularly at sunset. Fushimi Inari Shrine is famous for its tunnel of ten thousand vermilion torii gates climbing the mountain behind it; the full circuit takes about two hours and most visitors turn back before reaching the quieter upper sections.

Beyond the headline sights, smaller temples reward patient visitors. Tofuku-ji is exceptional in autumn for its maple-filled valley. Ryoan-ji houses Japan's most famous rock garden, fifteen stones arranged so that no single viewpoint reveals all of them simultaneously. Daitoku-ji is a complex of two dozen sub-temples, several of which are open to the public and almost always quiet. Saiho-ji, also known as the Moss Temple, requires advance reservation by postal mail and includes a brief sutra-copying session before garden access — the procedural friction keeps numbers low and the experience contemplative.

Walking is itself a major activity in Kyoto. The Philosopher's Path, a stone walkway following a canal between Ginkaku-ji and Nanzen-ji, is lined with cherry trees and small cafes, particularly atmospheric in spring or after rain. The preserved streets of Higashiyama, particularly Sannenzaka and Ninenzaka, retain Edo-period architecture and are best walked early in the morning before tour groups arrive. The Pontocho alley, running parallel to the Kamo River, transforms after dark into one of the city's best dining districts.

Active travellers can hike Mount Daimonji for a free panoramic city view — the trail starts behind Ginkaku-ji and takes about forty minutes up. The Kurama-Kibune mountain hike, in the northern hills, takes a half-day and includes a hot spring at the trailhead. Cycling along the Kamo River is flat, scenic, and especially pleasant in the early evening when locals gather along the banks.

Cultural experiences are plentiful but vary widely in authenticity and price. A traditional tea ceremony, ideally booked at a small private establishment rather than a hotel, runs about ¥3,000–6,000 and takes around 45 minutes. Kimono rental for a day of sightseeing is available throughout the Higashiyama and Gion districts and typically costs ¥3,000–5,000 including dressing. Geisha and maiko performances at Gion Corner offer a tourist-oriented introduction; the more authentic experience, an evening at an ochaya teahouse, is generally accessible only through introduction.

Day trips expand the options considerably. Nara, 45 minutes south by train, contains the great bronze Buddha of Todai-ji and a deer park where the resident deer have learned to bow for crackers. Osaka, 30 minutes away, is the food capital of Japan and an excellent counterpoint to Kyoto's restraint. The mountain monastery complex of Mount Hiei, accessible by cable car, was the seat of Tendai Buddhism for over a millennium.

Kyoto's seasonal events deserve specific planning. Gion Matsuri in July is one of Japan's three great festivals, with massive wooden floats paraded through the central streets. The Aoi Matsuri in May features a procession in Heian-period court costume. Autumn light-up events at major temples, where gardens are illuminated after sunset, run for two to three weeks in mid-November and require advance tickets at the more popular venues.
