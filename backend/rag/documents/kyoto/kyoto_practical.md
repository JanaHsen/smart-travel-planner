# Kyoto, Japan — Practical Information

Kyoto is moderately expensive by global standards but offers a wide spread of accommodation prices. Budget travellers can find dormitory beds in well-run hostels for ¥3,000–4,500 per night, and the city has an unusually high quality of capsule hotels in the ¥5,000–8,000 range. Mid-range business hotels and small boutique guesthouses run ¥10,000–18,000 per night for a double room. Traditional ryokan inns, with tatami floors, futon bedding, and multi-course kaiseki meals included, span an enormous range from around ¥18,000 to over ¥80,000 per person depending on the establishment. The most prestigious ryokan in the Higashiyama hills can exceed ¥100,000 per night and book out a year in advance during peak seasons.

Food costs are reasonable for the quality. A bowl of ramen or udon at a casual shop runs ¥800–1,200; a generous lunch set at a mid-range restaurant ¥1,500–2,500; and a full kaiseki dinner ¥8,000–25,000 depending on the venue. Convenience store meals, surprisingly good in Japan, can keep daily food spending under ¥2,000 if budget is the priority. Nishiki Market in central Kyoto offers a wide range of street snacks and is itself a worthwhile destination. Tipping is not practiced and would in most cases cause confusion; service is included in posted prices.

Transport within the city is handled by a combination of buses, two subway lines, and bicycles. The day pass for city buses costs ¥700 and pays for itself after three rides. The subway is faster but covers fewer of the major sights. Bicycle rental from the major hostels and dedicated rental shops costs ¥1,000–1,500 per day and is the fastest way to cover the temple districts; Kyoto is largely flat in the central basin. Taxis are clean and metered but expensive — a cross-city ride can easily exceed ¥3,000.

Getting to Kyoto from abroad almost always involves Kansai International Airport (KIX) near Osaka. The Haruka Express train runs direct to Kyoto Station in 75 minutes for around ¥3,400, or ¥1,800 with a foreign tourist discount available at the airport ticket counter. From Tokyo, the Tokaido Shinkansen is the fastest and most comfortable option at 2 hours 15 minutes; the Japan Rail Pass, available only to foreign visitors and purchased before arrival, makes long-distance rail travel substantially cheaper if multiple trips are planned.

Cash remains more important in Kyoto than in many comparable cities. While most hotels and chain restaurants accept credit cards, smaller restaurants, temples, and traditional shops are often cash-only. Convenience store ATMs at Seven-Eleven and Lawson reliably accept foreign cards. The local IC card, ICOCA, can be loaded with cash and used for transit and many small purchases; it is interoperable with the cards used in Tokyo and elsewhere.

Language is less of a barrier than first-time visitors fear. Major train stations, tourist sites, and hotels have English signage and staff with sufficient English to handle bookings and questions. Restaurants outside the tourist core may have only Japanese menus, but pointing at displayed plastic food models or other diners' meals is widely accepted. Translation apps work well for written menus.

Etiquette matters more in Kyoto than in many other Japanese cities, particularly around temples and the geisha districts. Shoes are removed when entering temple buildings; loud conversation is uncommon in shrine grounds; photographing geisha and maiko in Gion without permission is now formally prohibited and carries fines. Bathing in onsen requires washing thoroughly before entering the communal pool, and visible tattoos may be refused entry at traditional baths — though increasingly many establishments now accept tattooed guests.

Connectivity is excellent. Pocket WiFi rental from the airport runs about ¥1,000 per day; prepaid SIM cards are available for slightly less. Free WiFi is available at most cafes, train stations, and major tourist sites, though connections are sometimes time-limited. Power outlets accept the North American flat two-prong plug at 100 volts; most modern electronics handle this without adapters.
