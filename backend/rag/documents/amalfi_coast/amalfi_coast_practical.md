# Amalfi Coast, Italy — Practical Information

The Amalfi Coast is one of Italy's most expensive regions and budget accommodation is genuinely scarce, particularly in Positano. Hostel-style accommodation is essentially non-existent on the coast itself; the closest budget options are in Salerno or Sorrento, with daily ferry or bus connections to the coastal villages. Basic guesthouses and small B&Bs run €100–180 per night for a double room in shoulder season and €180–350 in peak summer. Mid-range hotels with sea views start around €250 per night in shoulder season and frequently exceed €500 in peak summer. The famous luxury properties — Le Sirenuse, Il San Pietro, Hotel Caruso — start around €1,200 per night in season and can exceed €5,000 for premium suites. Praiano and Cetara offer noticeably better value than Positano or Amalfi for comparable quality.

Food costs match the accommodation premium. A casual lunch — a focaccia, a simple pasta, a glass of wine — runs €25–40 per person in tourist areas. Mid-range dinner restaurants charge €60–100 per person for three courses with wine. The Michelin-starred restaurants of the coast, of which there are several, run €180–350 per person for tasting menus. Coffee at a stand-up bar costs €1.50–2.50; the same coffee at a table on a piazza costs €4–8 due to the table-service surcharge. Eating like a local — quick coffees standing at the bar, focaccia and pizza al taglio for lunch, fixed-menu dinners away from the main piazzas — can substantially reduce daily food costs.

Transport on the coast is the source of more visitor complaint than any other factor. The SITA bus network is the primary public transport, with frequent buses running the length of the SS163 between Sorrento, Positano, Amalfi, and Salerno. Tickets cost €1.30–4 depending on distance and must be purchased before boarding at tobacconists or vending machines. The buses are routinely packed in summer and frequently delayed by traffic; the standing-room-only conditions on hot afternoons are genuinely unpleasant. Ferry travel between villages is far more comfortable: tickets run €8–25 per leg and avoid the road traffic entirely. A combined ferry-and-walking strategy is the recommended way to experience the coast in summer.

Renting a car for the Amalfi Coast is a contentious topic. The road is genuinely difficult to drive — narrow, exposed, with inadequate passing places — and parking in the villages is severely limited and expensive (€20–50 per day at hotel and public lots). Visitors who want a car for surrounding-region exploration should consider basing in Sorrento or Salerno and dropping the car at the coast hotel's lot. Driving the coast purely as the experience is best done in shoulder season, ideally on a weekday, and ideally not as a first-time driver in Italy.

Scooter rental is popular among some visitors and disastrous for others. The combination of severe traffic, hairpin turns, and inexperienced summer riders produces a steady stream of accidents each season. Renters should be experienced motorcyclists in their home countries; an international driving permit is required and increasingly checked.

The Circumvesuviana train from Naples to Sorrento is the standard arrival route from the airport or train station. The 50-minute journey costs €4.90; the trains are old, unreserved, and notoriously crowded but reliable. From Sorrento, SITA buses or seasonal ferries connect to the Amalfi Coast proper. Naples Capodichino airport is the main international gateway; high-speed trains connect Rome to Naples in about 70 minutes for €30–60 booked in advance.

Cash and card acceptance is generally good. Most restaurants and hotels accept Visa and Mastercard; American Express is less reliably accepted. Smaller establishments, taxis, and beach clubs may be cash-preferred or cash-only. ATMs are widely available in Sorrento, Amalfi, and Positano but scarce in smaller villages. The euro is the local currency; non-euro currencies are not accepted by most businesses.

Connectivity is generally good in the villages but patchy on the road and trails. Most hotels, cafes, and restaurants offer reliable WiFi. EU roaming agreements mean that European Union visitors can use their domestic plans without surcharge. Visitors from outside the EU should arrange a tourist SIM at the airport or in Naples; coverage is reliable from Vodafone, TIM, and Wind on the major coastal routes.

Italian summer hours and the riposo (afternoon closure) catch some visitors unprepared. Many shops close from approximately 1 p.m. to 4 p.m. and reopen until 8 p.m. or later. Restaurant lunch service typically runs from noon to 2:30 p.m. and dinner from 7:30 to 10:30 p.m. Sunday closures of small shops are common. Major sights and ferries operate continuously through the day.

Italy's standard tourist visa rules apply — Schengen Area entry for most Western nationalities, with up to 90 days of visa-free travel within any 180-day period. The coast is well-policed and tourist-safe; petty theft is the main risk and concentrated in the buses and crowded village squares.
