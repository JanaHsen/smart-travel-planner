# Amalfi Coast, Italy — Overview

The Amalfi Coast is a 50-kilometre stretch of coastline along the southern edge of the Sorrentine Peninsula in Italy's Campania region, south of Naples and Salerno. Designated a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1997, the coast is defined by terraced cliffs falling sharply into the Tyrrhenian Sea, dotted with pastel-coloured villages built into the rock face — Positano, Amalfi, Ravello, Praiano, and a dozen smaller settlements. The region has been a destination for wealthy travellers since the early 19th century, when British and German Romantic artists popularized the coast in their paintings; it remains one of Italy's most expensive and most photographed destinations.

The geography produces both the coast's beauty and its logistical difficulty. A single road, the Strada Statale 163, runs the length of the coastline carved into the cliffside, with hairpin turns, narrow sections that barely accommodate two-way traffic, and frequent bottlenecks at the village entrances. In peak summer this road becomes essentially gridlocked from mid-morning until evening, transforming what should be 30-minute drives into multi-hour ordeals. The coast is far more pleasant approached on foot, by ferry, or outside the peak months.

The climate is classic Mediterranean, with hot dry summers and mild damp winters. July and August see daytime highs of 30–32°C, very low rainfall, and the warmest sea temperatures around 25°C — these are also the months with by far the highest prices and worst crowding. May, June, September, and early October offer near-ideal conditions: 22–28°C daytime temperatures, swimmable seas through October, and significantly thinner crowds. November through March is the off-season; many hotels, restaurants, and ferry routes close entirely, but the coast retains a quiet beauty for visitors interested in the villages rather than swimming.

Best time to visit comes down to a tradeoff between weather and crowd tolerance. Early to mid-June and the second half of September are widely considered optimal — warm enough for the sea, before or after the peak Italian school holidays, and with most of the seasonal businesses still operating. May has occasional rain but pleasant temperatures and the lowest peak-season prices. July and August should be avoided unless beach weather is essential and crowds are acceptable; even then, weekday visits and early-morning sightseeing are strongly preferable.

The villages each have a distinct character. Positano is the most visually iconic, climbing in a vertical jumble from a small beach to the town proper hundreds of metres above; it is also the most expensive and the most touristed. Amalfi, the historic centre that gives the coast its name, is larger and has a substantial cathedral and waterfront piazza; its prices are slightly more reasonable than Positano's. Ravello sits high in the hills above the coast, away from the beach scene, and is known for its gardens, classical music festival, and a cooler quieter atmosphere. Praiano, between Positano and Amalfi, is smaller and quieter and offers some of the best value accommodation. Cetara, further east, retains a working fishing village character largely lost in the more famous towns.

Sorrento, often confused with the Amalfi Coast, sits on the opposite side of the peninsula and is technically not part of the coast itself. Many visitors base themselves in Sorrento for ease of transport and visit the Amalfi Coast as day trips; the trade-off is slightly more touristy infrastructure but vastly easier logistics.

Distances on the coast are short but travel times are long. Positano to Amalfi is 16 kilometres but takes 30–60 minutes by bus or car depending on traffic. Naples to Sorrento is 50 minutes by Circumvesuviana train; Sorrento to Positano is 45–60 minutes by SITA bus. Ferries between the major coastal towns and to Capri operate from approximately April through October and are by far the most pleasant transport mode in season — both for the views and for avoiding road traffic.
