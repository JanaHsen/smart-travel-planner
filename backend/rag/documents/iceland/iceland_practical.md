# Iceland — Practical Information

Accommodation in Iceland is genuinely expensive and budget options are limited. Hostel dorm beds run 5,000–9,000 ISK ($35–65) per night; private rooms at hostels and budget guesthouses 13,000–22,000 ISK ($95–160). Mid-range hotels and well-reviewed guesthouses run 25,000–40,000 ISK ($180–290) per night for a double room. Boutique hotels and upmarket countryside lodges cost 40,000–80,000 ISK ($290–580). The high-end tier — properties like the Retreat at Blue Lagoon, Deplar Farm, the Highland Base — runs 80,000–250,000+ ISK per night. Peak summer (mid-June to mid-August) sees prices roughly 30–50% above shoulder rates and requires booking 4–6 months ahead at popular properties. Self-catering accommodation through Airbnb and similar platforms remains widely available outside Reykjavík; the platform's restrictions in the capital have tightened.

Camping is a legitimate budget option in summer. The country has an extensive network of organized campgrounds with facilities, charging 1,500–3,000 ISK per person per night; rough wild camping has been restricted in recent years and is now formally limited to specific permitted areas. The Camping Card, valid at participating campgrounds across the country, costs around €175 for unlimited summer camping for two adults and up to four children — exceptional value for extended camping itineraries.

Food costs are high. A casual meal — soup of the day with bread, sandwich, or simple plate — runs 2,500–4,500 ISK ($18–35) per person. Restaurant dinners cost 5,000–9,000 ISK ($35–65) per main course; high-end restaurants charge 12,000–25,000+ ISK ($85–180) for main courses or 18,000–30,000 ISK for tasting menus. Self-catering with groceries from Bónus or Krónan supermarkets reduces food costs substantially — a basic grocery shop for a few days' meals runs 8,000–15,000 ISK ($60–110) per person. Alcohol is severely restricted in price and availability — beer in restaurants runs 1,400–2,200 ISK ($10–16) per glass; the state alcohol monopoly Vínbúðin operates limited-hour liquor stores and is the cheaper purchase channel; supermarkets do not sell anything stronger than 2.25% beer. The famous duty-free shop on arrival at Keflavík airport is by a wide margin the cheapest place to purchase alcohol in Iceland and is heavily used by Icelanders themselves.

Transport for visitors centres on rental cars for nearly all itineraries beyond Reykjavík and the immediate surroundings. Daily rental rates run $80–200 for a small car in summer; 4x4 vehicles required for the F-roads (interior highlands) cost $150–350 per day. Mandatory insurance, gravel protection coverage, and sand/ash protection coverage add $30–60 per day depending on the chosen package; these are practical necessities given Iceland's road conditions, not optional extras. Petrol costs 320–380 ISK per litre ($8.50–10.50 per US gallon equivalent), making fuel a significant trip expense — a Ring Road circuit consumes around 100,000–140,000 ISK ($720–1,000) in fuel for a small car.

Public transport outside Reykjavík is limited. Strætó buses run between major towns but with infrequent service that is impractical for tourist itineraries. Coach tours offer the alternative for non-driving visitors, with day tours from Reykjavík covering the Golden Circle, South Coast highlights, and aurora viewing trips. Multi-day group tours cover the longer routes; pricing runs $1,500–4,000 per person for a Ring Road tour. Domestic flights from Reykjavík's city airport (not Keflavík) connect to Akureyri, Egilsstaðir, Ísafjörður, and the Westman Islands at around $80–180 one way; these reduce the long drives to short flights and are practical for visitors with limited time targeting specific regions.

Keflavík International Airport (KEF), 50 kilometres southwest of Reykjavík, handles all international flights. The Flybus shuttle to the city centre runs in coordination with arrivals and departures at $35 one way; private transfers and rental car pickups are alternatives. Direct flights from major North American cities (New York, Boston, Chicago, Toronto, Denver) run 5–7 hours; from European hubs (London, Amsterdam, Frankfurt, Copenhagen) flights run 3–4 hours. Icelandair's stopover programme allows visitors to break a transatlantic journey in Iceland for up to seven days at no extra airfare.

Cards are universally accepted across Iceland — the country is essentially cashless for visitors. Visa, Mastercard, contactless payment, and major mobile wallets work everywhere from gas stations to museums to small countryside guesthouses. Cash is rarely needed; ATMs are available in towns but most visitors complete entire trips without ever withdrawing Icelandic króna. Credit cards with PIN are required at unattended fuel stations and parking machines — chip-and-PIN-capable cards or pre-trip arrangements with the issuing bank are essential. The 25.5% VAT applies to most purchases; tourists can claim VAT refunds on goods exceeding 6,000 ISK from a single shop on departure.

Connectivity is excellent throughout populated areas and along the major highways; remote interior sections of the Ring Road and the highlands have spotty or absent coverage. Tourist SIMs from Síminn or Vodafone Iceland cost $20–40 for multi-week packages with substantial data; coverage from Síminn is generally the most extensive. WiFi is standard at hotels, guesthouses, restaurants, and visitor centres.

Weather preparation is the most consequential single planning factor. Iceland's weather changes rapidly and dramatically; a clear morning can become a horizontal-rain afternoon within an hour, particularly in the south and east. Genuinely waterproof outer layers (Gore-Tex or equivalent), warm mid-layers (fleece or synthetic insulation), proper hiking boots, and wind-blocking gear are essential rather than optional even in peak summer. Winter visitors need significantly heavier outerwear and proper crampons or microspikes for the routinely icy walking conditions. Quality outdoor gear is available in Reykjavík from 66°North, Cintamani, and the major outdoor retailers but at a substantial markup over Northern Hemisphere prices.

Driving conditions deserve specific attention. Single-lane bridges, gravel sections, sudden weather changes, blowing sand and ash, and aggressive seasonal wind are routine challenges that catch unprepared drivers off guard. The Vegagerðin (road authority) website's road condition maps should be consulted daily during winter and shoulder seasons; the SafeTravel service provides specific weather and road warnings. Off-road driving is illegal everywhere outside designated routes — even briefly straying onto vegetated ground for a photo can incur substantial fines and causes lasting damage in Iceland's slow-growing ecosystems.

Visa requirements for most Western nationalities are straightforward — Iceland is a Schengen Area member, visa-free entry up to 90 days within any 180-day period for visa-exempt travellers. Iceland is not an EU member but is part of the Schengen and EEA agreements.

Health considerations are minor. Tap water is exceptional and should be drunk freely from the tap; bottled water is unnecessary. Geothermal water in showers smells of sulphur and is harmless. Standard travel insurance is recommended given the high cost of any private medical care; the Icelandic public system provides emergency care to visitors but at full cost.
