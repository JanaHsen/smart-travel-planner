# Iceland — Things to Do

The Golden Circle is the most popular short-trip route and is achievable as a long day trip from Reykjavík. Þingvellir National Park, the first stop, is a UNESCO World Heritage site for its historical significance — the original meeting site of the Alþingi, one of the world's oldest continuous parliaments, founded in 930 — and for its geological position at the visible boundary of the North American and Eurasian tectonic plates, where visitors can walk between the diverging cliff faces. The Silfra fissure within the park is one of the world's premier dive and snorkel sites; the gin-clear glacial water provides over 100 metres of visibility through a narrow rift between the plates, with water temperatures of 2–4°C requiring full dry suits. The Geysir geothermal area, the second stop, is the source of the English word "geyser" — Strokkur erupts every 5–10 minutes in dependable bursts of 15–20 metres. Gullfoss, the third stop, is a two-tier waterfall plunging into a deep canyon and is the country's most photographed waterfall after Skógafoss.

The South Coast, running east from Reykjavík along Route 1, contains an exceptional density of major attractions accessible from the highway. Seljalandsfoss is a 60-metre waterfall with a path running behind the falling water — wet but memorable. Skógafoss is a single 60-metre wide waterfall with a tall viewing staircase to the upper rim. The black sand beach at Reynisfjara, with its basalt sea stacks and columnar cliffs, is justifiably famous and treacherously dangerous in rough weather; the "sneaker waves" that have killed multiple visitors arrive without warning and visitors should heed local signage and stay well back from the waterline. The Vík village marks roughly the midway point of the south coast; further east, the Skaftafell area within Vatnajökull National Park offers glacier hikes, ice climbing, and access to the famous Skaftafell waterfall. The Jökulsárlón glacier lagoon, with floating icebergs calved from Breiðamerkurjökull glacier, and the adjacent Diamond Beach, where icebergs wash up on black sand, are the most photogenic single sites in the country.

Glacier hiking and ice cave tours are signature experiences. Glacier hikes run year-round on Sólheimajökull and Falljökull, with crampons, ice axes, and certified guides included in the standard $90–150 per person rates. Multi-day glacier expeditions, including ascents of the highest peaks (Hvannadalshnjúkur at 2,110 metres), are available for experienced mountaineers. Natural ice caves form within the glaciers from approximately November through March; the famous blue ice caves of Vatnajökull are accessible only by guided tour at $200–350 per person, with the tours dependent on annual ice conditions and not bookable until each season's caves form.

The aurora borealis viewing season runs from late August through mid-April. The aurora is essentially a clear-skies-and-solar-activity phenomenon — the displays themselves happen continuously around the polar regions during dark hours, but cloud cover is the limiting factor for ground viewing. Aurora forecasts integrate solar activity and cloud cover predictions; the Icelandic Met Office's aurora forecast and several specialty apps provide reliable nightly outlooks. Self-driving from Reykjavík to a dark-sky location (typically 30–60 minutes outside the city to escape light pollution) is the standard approach for independent visitors. Aurora bus tours run nightly during the season; reputable operators offer free re-bookings if no aurora is seen.

Whale watching from Húsavík on the north coast is some of the most reliable in Europe, with peak season from May through September. Resident species include humpback, minke, white-beaked dolphin, and harbour porpoise; orcas, blue whales, and fin whales appear seasonally. The smaller boats out of Húsavík provide a more intimate experience than the large catamarans operating from Reykjavík harbour. Whale watching from Reykjavík is reliable May through September with lower species variety; tours run year-round.

Reykjavík itself rewards two days of exploration. Hallgrímskirkja, the dramatic concrete church inspired by basalt columns, is the city's signature building and offers the best urban panorama from its tower. The National Museum and the Settlement Exhibition cover Icelandic history from the Viking age forward. Harpa concert hall, with its honeycomb glass facade designed by Olafur Eliasson, is the country's premier performing arts venue and worth visiting for the architecture alone. The food scene runs from the famous hot dog stand Bæjarins Beztu Pylsur (where Bill Clinton ate during his presidency) through high-end restaurants serving New Nordic interpretations of Icelandic ingredients — fermented shark for the curious, fresh langoustine and Arctic char for the more conventional. The Laugardalslaug municipal swimming pool and the upscale Sky Lagoon spa both offer the iconic Icelandic geothermal bathing experience without the crowds and pricing of the famous Blue Lagoon.

The Blue Lagoon, 50 minutes from Reykjavík and 20 minutes from the airport, is the country's most famous attraction and consequently its most touristed. The mineral-rich geothermal waters, milky blue from silica content, are genuinely pleasant; the venue's design and the silica face masks included in entry are well-executed. Pricing has risen substantially — basic admission now runs around €60 per person and routinely sells out days in advance during peak season. Many travellers prefer the alternatives: the Sky Lagoon in Reykjavík, the Mývatn Nature Baths in the north, the Krauma baths near Reykholt, or the simple municipal swimming pools that locals use daily.

Adventure activities include glacier hikes (mentioned above), ice climbing, snowmobiling on the glaciers, snowshoeing, dog sledding (winter), super-jeep tours into the highlands, river rafting, and lava cave exploration. The interior highlands, accessible only in summer via F-roads requiring 4x4 vehicles, contain the country's most dramatic landscapes — Landmannalaugar's rhyolite mountains, Þórsmörk's valleys, and the route between them which serves as the famous Laugavegur multi-day trekking route.

The Ring Road around the country is itself a defining activity. A complete circle from Reykjavík covers 1,332 kilometres but realistically requires 7–14 days to allow proper exploration of the major sites. The route splits naturally into the South Coast (days 1–3), the Eastfjords (day 4), Lake Mývatn and the north (days 5–7), the Westfjords detour or Snæfellsnes Peninsula (days 8–10), and return to Reykjavík. Many visitors do partial circles — the popular "South Coast and back" runs as 3–5 days; the "Snæfellsnes weekend" runs 2–3 days from Reykjavík.
