# Iceland — Overview

Iceland is a North Atlantic island nation roughly the size of England, sitting just south of the Arctic Circle on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge — the boundary between the North American and Eurasian tectonic plates. The active geology produces a defining landscape of glaciers, volcanoes, geothermal fields, lava plateaus, glacial rivers, and waterfalls; roughly 11% of the country is covered by glaciers and around 30 active volcanic systems lie within its borders. The population of around 390,000, two-thirds of whom live in the Reykjavík metropolitan area, makes Iceland one of Europe's most sparsely populated countries; vast stretches of the interior are uninhabited and largely roadless.

The country was settled by Norwegian and Celtic immigrants in the late 9th and 10th centuries and the medieval Icelandic Sagas — written in Old Norse during the 13th and 14th centuries — represent one of medieval Europe's most distinctive literary traditions. Iceland declared full independence from Denmark in 1944 and has since become one of the world's most prosperous and socially progressive nations, consistently ranking near the top of indices measuring gender equality, civic stability, and quality of life.

The climate is significantly milder than the latitude would suggest, owing to the warming influence of the Gulf Stream. Summer (June–August) sees daytime temperatures of 10–15°C and the famous near-continuous daylight of the high latitudes; the sun barely sets in late June, with bright "midnight" twilight rather than darkness. Winter (December–February) brings short days (4–5 hours of daylight in late December), temperatures generally between -5 and 5°C in Reykjavík, and significant snow throughout much of the country. Wind is the more consequential weather factor than temperature; the saying "if you don't like the weather, wait five minutes" is a near-literal description of conditions in much of the country. The interior highlands are inaccessible by road for roughly two-thirds of the year (typically late September through mid-June).

Best time to visit depends heavily on priorities. June through August is peak season — long daylight, accessible interior highlands, all roads and routes open, but with high prices and significant tourist volumes at the most famous sites. May and September are strong shoulder months with reasonable weather, much lower visitor numbers, and the chance for early or late aurora sightings. October through March is the aurora borealis viewing season, with peak conditions on cold clear nights from December through February; this is also the season for ice cave tours, which require winter freeze conditions. November and February tend to offer the best balance of aurora opportunity, accessible roads, and reasonable prices.

Iceland is one of the world's most expensive countries to visit and budget-conscious travellers should plan accordingly. Daily costs of $200–400 are routine for comfortable mid-range travel; budget travellers using guesthouse accommodation, self-catering, and avoiding paid activities can manage at $120–180 per day. The country's tourism boom of the past decade has produced substantial increases in capacity and competition, but the underlying cost structure — high wages, imported goods, geographic isolation — keeps prices high. The exchange rate of the Icelandic króna against the dollar and euro has fluctuated significantly; checking current rates near the trip date matters.

Reykjavík is the country's only major city and serves as the base for most visits. The capital is small enough to walk end-to-end in 45 minutes but has a disproportionately strong restaurant scene, well-funded cultural institutions, and a famously vibrant nightlife centred on the bar circuit of Laugavegur and the surrounding streets. The Golden Circle — comprising Þingvellir National Park, the Geysir geothermal area, and Gullfoss waterfall — sits within a 90-minute drive of the city and is the most popular short-trip itinerary. The South Coast, running along Route 1 from Reykjavík toward the southeast, contains a sequence of waterfalls, black sand beaches, and the Vatnajökull glacier. The full Ring Road around the country, 1,332 kilometres of paved highway, is the standard route for week-long-plus visits; driving the complete circle takes minimum five days but rewards 8–14.

Best time to visit also includes specific natural events worth planning around. The midnight sun period in late June produces a distinctive experience and is the only time the interior highlands open. The aurora borealis season runs from late August through mid-April, with peak conditions roughly October through March; aurora forecasts are reliable but viewing always depends on cloud cover. Whale watching in the north (Húsavík especially) peaks May through September. Puffin nesting season runs roughly mid-May through mid-August on the Westman Islands, the Westfjords, and Látrabjarg.
