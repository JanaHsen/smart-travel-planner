# Bali, Indonesia — Things to Do

Surfing is foundational to Bali's tourism identity and the variety of breaks suits every experience level. Kuta and Legian beaches offer beginner-friendly waves with extensive surf school infrastructure; group lessons run $25–45. Canggu — Echo Beach, Batu Bolong, Berawa — has emerged as the central hub for intermediate surfers, with consistent waves, a vast surfboard rental market, and a robust cafe-and-restaurant scene catering to the surf community. The famous Bukit Peninsula breaks of Uluwatu, Padang Padang, Bingin, and Impossibles are world-class advanced waves working best in dry season; these are reef breaks with serious consequences for poor judgment and not suitable for inexperienced surfers. The east coast breaks (Keramas, Sanur reef) work best in wet season.

Yoga, wellness retreats, and Vipassana-style meditation centres represent another major draw, concentrated in Ubud but distributed throughout the island. The Yoga Barn in Ubud is the largest and best-known facility, offering drop-in classes ($12–18) in multiple styles and multi-day retreats. Smaller boutique studios offer more specialized programs in everything from Iyengar to Kundalini to ecstatic dance. Multi-day silent retreats, plant medicine ceremonies, and breathwork programs have proliferated; quality and ethical standing vary widely and researching specific operators carefully is essential.

The Ubud cultural circuit covers temple visits, traditional dance performances, and rice terrace landscapes within a half-day to full-day itinerary. The Tegallalang Rice Terraces, the most photographed in Bali, are now genuinely overcrowded but remain visually striking; visiting at sunrise (before 7 a.m.) provides a more reasonable experience. The Sacred Monkey Forest sanctuary in central Ubud houses several troops of long-tailed macaques among ancient temple ruins; the monkeys are habituated and theft of bags, phones, and sunglasses is routine. Saraswati Temple's lotus pond and the multiple smaller temples around the central town are quieter alternatives. Traditional dance performances — Legong, Barong, the famous Kecak fire dance at Uluwatu Temple at sunset — run nightly across multiple venues at $10–20 per person.

Temple visits define much of the cultural experience. Tanah Lot, the dramatic offshore temple silhouetted against sunsets on the west coast, is the most photographed temple in Bali and accordingly its busiest in the late afternoon. Uluwatu Temple, on a 70-metre cliff above the southern ocean, combines temple architecture with dramatic geography and the daily Kecak performance. Tirta Empul, the holy spring temple in central Bali, allows visitors to participate in the traditional bathing purification ritual under monks' supervision. Pura Lempuyang's "Gates of Heaven" became famous through Instagram and the resulting queues for the iconic photograph routinely exceed an hour; the multi-temple complex around Lempuyang offers more rewarding experiences for visitors willing to climb beyond the famous gate. Sarong rental is required at all temples and typically included in entry fees ($2–8).

Mount Batur sunrise hike is the standard adventure outing — a 4 a.m. start, two-hour ascent of the active 1,717-metre volcano in the central highlands, sunrise from the rim with the larger Mount Agung visible in the distance, and descent for breakfast cooked over volcanic steam vents. Tour operators handle the logistics from any major tourist area; pricing runs $35–70 per person including transport, guide, and breakfast. The hike requires reasonable fitness but no technical skill; warm layers are essential as the summit is genuinely cold before sunrise.

Diving on the east coast is exceptional and underrated. Tulamben features the wreck of the USS Liberty, a World War II American cargo ship sunk by a Japanese submarine in 1942; the wreck sits 30 metres from shore, accessible from the beach, and is one of the most diveable wrecks in Asia. Amed offers excellent reef diving with Japanese WWII wrecks and consistent macro-life subjects. Menjangan Island in West Bali National Park has the country's healthiest coral and the deepest visibility. The Nusa Islands are popular for manta ray diving at Manta Point off Nusa Penida and the rare mola mola (oceanic sunfish) at Crystal Bay during cooler months (July–October). PADI dive courses run at all major dive bases; certified diver day trips cost $80–140 for two-tank dives including equipment.

The Nusa Islands deserve their own dedicated trip, typically 2–3 nights, and offer some of Bali's most dramatic coastal landscapes. Nusa Penida is the largest and most visited, famous for the cliff viewpoint of Kelingking Beach (the "T-Rex shaped" promontory), Angel's Billabong, and Crystal Bay. Nusa Lembongan, the smallest, has a more developed tourism infrastructure with beach clubs and good snorkeling. Nusa Ceningan is connected to Lembongan by a yellow suspension bridge and offers a quieter atmosphere with fewer organized tours. Fast boats from Sanur run multiple times daily at $25–40 round trip.

Cooking classes have become a near-mandatory cultural experience for many visitors. Most include a market visit, instruction in 4–6 traditional Balinese dishes, and the meal at the end. Half-day classes run $35–60 per person; full-day immersions with multiple courses cost $60–100. The schools associated with Paon Bali in Ubud and several smaller operators in Canggu and Seminyak are consistently well-reviewed.

Spa treatments are exceptional value. A traditional Balinese massage runs $8–18 at neighbourhood spas; high-end resort spas charge $50–150 for elaborate multi-treatment packages. Multi-hour spa days incorporating massage, body scrubs, flower baths, and traditional ceremonies are widely offered at $80–250.

The waterfall circuit in central and northern Bali — Tegenungan near Ubud, Sekumpul in the north, Tukad Cepung's cave waterfall, and the Aling-Aling complex — has emerged as a popular activity. Most waterfalls require moderate hikes and modest entry fees ($2–8). Sekumpul, the most spectacular, is a multi-tier system requiring a guide for the descent; bookings can be arranged on arrival.
