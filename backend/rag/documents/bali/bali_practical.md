# Bali, Indonesia — Practical Information

Accommodation in Bali offers exceptional value across all price points. Hostel dorm beds run 100,000–250,000 IDR ($6.50–16) per night; private rooms in hostels and budget guesthouses 250,000–500,000 IDR ($16–32). Mid-range hotels and guesthouses with pools cost 500,000–1,500,000 IDR ($32–95) per night for a double. Boutique hotels, well-positioned villas, and the famous riverside resorts of Ubud run 1,500,000–4,500,000 IDR ($95–290). The high-end tier — Four Seasons Sayan and Jimbaran Bay, Bulgari Uluwatu, Como Shambhala, the Nihi Sumba experiences — runs 4,500,000–25,000,000+ IDR per night. Private villa rental, with a personal pool and often a small staff, is widely available across all price ranges and frequently better value than equivalent hotel pricing for groups of 4+. Peak season (July, August, late December through early January) sees prices roughly 30–60% above shoulder rates.

Food costs are remarkably low. A complete meal at a warung — the small family-run local restaurants serving nasi goreng, mie goreng, and the standard Indonesian repertoire — runs 25,000–60,000 IDR ($1.60–4) per person. Mid-range restaurants in tourist areas charge 100,000–250,000 IDR ($6.50–16) per main course. Upscale international restaurants in Seminyak, Canggu, and Ubud charge 300,000–700,000 IDR ($19–45) per main course. The famous Bali coffee culture supports both inexpensive local kopi and elaborate third-wave specialty cafes; specialty coffee runs 35,000–75,000 IDR. Fresh tropical fruit at local markets is exceptional and inexpensive. Tipping is appreciated but not expected at the levels of Western countries; rounding up at warungs and 5–10% at upscale restaurants is the local convention.

Transport options include scooter rental, hired drivers, ride-hailing apps, and taxis. Scooter rental ($5–10 per day) is the most popular mode among visitors; the road conditions, traffic density, and aggressive driving culture make this genuinely dangerous for inexperienced riders, and serious injuries are routine among visitors who underestimate the difficulty. International driving permits with motorcycle endorsement are required and increasingly enforced through police checkpoints in tourist areas; helmets are required and frequently checked. Visitors without prior motorcycle experience should choose alternatives.

Hired drivers — typically with their own car, hourly or full-day — are the standard mode for sightseeing and longer journeys. Full-day driver services run 600,000–1,000,000 IDR ($38–65) for 8–10 hours including the vehicle and fuel, often less than the cost of multiple Grab rides for the same itinerary. Many drivers serve as informal guides and are repeatedly hired across multi-day visits. Drivers can be arranged through accommodation, on the street in tourist areas, or through specific WhatsApp networks among the long-stay community.

Grab and Gojek, the major ride-hailing apps, work reliably in tourist areas; rides within central neighbourhoods cost 15,000–50,000 IDR ($1–3.50) and represent excellent value compared to negotiated taxi fares. Conventional taxis use the Bluebird brand, the most reliable; insisting on the meter and avoiding the negotiated-fare alternative is the standard advice. The ride-hailing apps experience occasional friction with traditional taxi drivers in some areas; the apps usually function but pickup locations may be restricted.

Air travel handles all international arrivals through Ngurah Rai International Airport (DPS), 12 kilometres south of Denpasar in Tuban. Direct flights connect to all major Asian hubs — Singapore, Kuala Lumpur, Hong Kong, Tokyo, Seoul, Beijing — and increasingly to Australian cities (Sydney, Melbourne, Perth) and Middle Eastern hubs (Dubai, Doha) for European connections. Domestic flights to Java (Jakarta, Yogyakarta, Surabaya), Lombok, and other Indonesian islands run frequently. Pre-arranged airport transfers cost 250,000–400,000 IDR ($16–26) to Seminyak/Canggu, 600,000–800,000 IDR ($40–50) to Ubud. Grab from the airport requires walking to the designated pickup zone outside the regulated taxi area.

Cards are accepted at hotels, mid-range and upscale restaurants, and tourist-oriented shops in established areas. Smaller restaurants, warungs, markets, taxis, and most rural businesses are cash-only. ATMs are plentiful in tourist areas; foreign card withdrawal limits are typically 1,250,000–2,500,000 IDR per transaction with fees of 25,000–50,000 IDR. The Indonesian rupiah has many zeroes that frequently confuse visitors; building familiarity with the conversion rate during the first day is helpful. Currency exchange is widely available; using authorized exchange offices (look for "PVA Berizin" certification) rather than street exchanges avoids the substantial counting-trick scams that have historically operated in tourist areas.

Connectivity is excellent in tourist areas and the digital nomad infrastructure has driven significant investment in coworking spaces, fibre internet at cafes, and reliable mobile data. Tourist SIMs from Telkomsel, XL Axiata, or Indosat cost 100,000–200,000 IDR for multi-week packages with substantial data; coverage from Telkomsel is the most extensive. WiFi is universal at hotels, restaurants, and the major coworking spaces of Canggu, Ubud, and Sanur.

Health considerations include mosquito-borne illnesses (dengue is endemic; standard repellent precautions apply, particularly during wet season), traveller's diarrhea (common; bottled water, food hygiene awareness, and standard precautions reduce risk), and Bali belly — the colloquial term for gastrointestinal illness affecting many visitors regardless of their precautions. Carrying basic medications including loperamide and oral rehydration salts is the practical approach. Dengue is the most serious routine health risk; yellow fever vaccination is not required for most visitors. Tap water is not potable; bottled water is cheap and ubiquitous.

Safety considerations in Bali are generally moderate. Petty theft, particularly bag-snatching by passing motorcyclists, has been an issue in busy tourist areas; carrying bags away from the road side and avoiding obvious displays of valuables substantially reduces risk. Scooter accidents represent the largest single category of serious visitor injuries. Beach swimming requires attention to local conditions; rip currents at the popular south coast beaches have caused deaths and the warning flag system (red flag = no swimming) should be heeded.

Visa requirements have shifted significantly in recent years. Most Western nationalities can use the Visa on Arrival (VOA) system, currently 500,000 IDR ($32) for a 30-day permit extendable once. The newer 60-day Visa B211A and the 5-year "Second Home" visa have created additional options for longer stays. The free 30-day visa-exempt scheme has been suspended since 2020 and replaced by the paid VOA. Indonesian visa policy changes regularly; checking current requirements within a month of travel is essential.

Sustainable tourism considerations have become more visible. The mass tourism economy strains the island's water resources, waste management infrastructure, and traditional cultural patterns. Visitors making conscious choices — using refillable water bottles, choosing operators with verifiable sustainable practices, respecting temple etiquette and local ceremonies, and supporting traditional villages and small businesses — meaningfully reduce these impacts.
