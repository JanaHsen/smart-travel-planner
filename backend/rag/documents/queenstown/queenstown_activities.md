# Queenstown, New Zealand — Things to Do

Bungee jumping was commercialized in Queenstown in 1988 and the original Kawarau Bridge site, 23 kilometres east of town, remains operating today. The 43-metre jump above the Kawarau River is the entry-level option; the Nevis Highwire, at 134 metres above the Nevis River, is the highest in New Zealand and one of the highest commercial jumps in the world. The Ledge Bungy operates after dark in winter for night jumps with the town lit up below. Combo packages bundle multiple jumps with skydiving, jet boating, and other activities at a discount over individual booking.

Skydiving is similarly extensive, with multiple operators running tandem jumps from 9,000, 12,000, and 15,000 feet. The drop zones offer some of the most scenic skydiving in the world — the lake, the Remarkables, and on clear days a view extending to the Southern Alps. Jumps run year-round but weather cancellations are routine; building flexibility into the schedule is essential. Costs run NZ$300–500 depending on altitude.

The Shotover Jet operates in the steep canyons of the Shotover River and is the original commercial jet boat experience that the region pioneered. The 25-minute trips run year-round, reaching speeds of 85 kilometres per hour through gaps barely wider than the boat itself. KJet operates a longer river-and-lake tour at slower speeds, suitable for visitors who want the experience without the white-knuckle intensity. Costs run NZ$160–200 per person.

Skiing in winter is centred on four mountains. Coronet Peak, 25 minutes from the town centre, is the closest and most accessible; it offers night skiing on Wednesdays through Saturdays and is the region's most family-friendly mountain. The Remarkables, 45 minutes away, is generally regarded as the better mountain for advanced skiers and snowboarders, with steeper terrain and a higher base elevation. Cardrona, 60 minutes away over the Crown Range, is the largest ski area and is particularly popular for freestyle and park riding. Treble Cone, 75 minutes near Wanaka, has the longest vertical descent in New Zealand and the most challenging terrain. Day passes run NZ$130–180; multi-mountain passes offer better value for visitors planning multiple ski days.

Heli-skiing in the surrounding ranges is a marquee experience for advanced skiers. Multiple operators run day trips with three to six helicopter drops in untouched powder; pricing starts around NZ$1,400 per person and depends on the conditions and the run package. Booking flexibility is essential as flights cancel routinely for weather.

Hiking options range from gentle lakeside walks to multi-day Great Walks. The Queenstown Hill walking track, starting from town, takes 2–3 hours round trip and reaches a 900-metre summit with panoramic views; the Tiki Trail and Ben Lomond Track offer progressively more demanding alternatives starting from the same trailhead. The Routeburn Track, one of New Zealand's nine Great Walks, runs 32 kilometres over 2–4 days through alpine country and ancient beech forest. Booking for the Routeburn opens in May for the following season and books out within hours; alternative Great Walks like the Caples and Greenstone tracks offer comparable scenery without the booking pressure.

The wine country of Central Otago is now globally recognized for its Pinot Noir. The Gibbston Valley, 25 minutes from Queenstown, is the closest cluster of cellar doors and includes Gibbston Valley Wines, Peregrine, and several smaller producers. The Bannockburn area near Cromwell, 50 minutes away, holds the densest concentration of small-production Pinot Noir specialists. Wine tour operators run group day tours by minivan with lunch included; private guided tours suit smaller groups and allow more flexibility on the itinerary.

Arrowtown, 25 minutes from Queenstown, is a preserved gold-rush settlement with a small but excellent regional museum, well-restored historic main street, and the Bush Creek Chinese settlement remains. The town is most photogenic in autumn when the surrounding poplar and willow trees turn gold; the Arrowtown Autumn Festival in late April draws substantial visitor numbers.

Boat trips on Lake Wakatipu range from short scenic cruises to the historic TSS Earnslaw, a coal-fired steamship that has run on the lake since 1912. The Earnslaw makes daily crossings to Walter Peak high country station, where a sheep-shearing demonstration and farm-style barbecue lunch operate. Smaller operators run kayaking, paddle boarding, and water-skiing on the lake during summer.

Mountain biking in Queenstown has developed into a world-class scene. The Queenstown Bike Park on Skyline operates year-round with a gondola lift, with extensive downhill and cross-country networks at all skill levels. The Queenstown Trail, a 110-kilometre lakeside and riverside network connecting Arrowtown, Gibbston, and Jacks Point, provides excellent leisure cycling for visitors not interested in technical riding. Bike rental is widely available; e-bikes have particularly transformed the trail-cycling experience for casual riders.

Day trips to Milford Sound from Queenstown run as long bus-and-cruise packages — leaving around 7 a.m. and returning around 8 p.m., with most of the time spent on the road. The cruise itself, 2 hours through fjord landscape with thousand-metre cliffs and frequent waterfalls, is justifiably famous. Visitors with greater flexibility should consider basing in Te Anau for one or two nights to allow earlier morning cruises with better light and fewer crowds.
