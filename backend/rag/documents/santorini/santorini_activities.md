# Santorini, Greece — Things to Do

The Oia sunset is Santorini's most famous activity and the source of much of the island's contemporary fame. The village's western orientation produces stunning evening light against the whitewashed buildings; thousands of visitors gather along the cliff walkways and at specific viewpoints to watch the sun drop behind the small island of Therasia across the caldera. The crowds are genuinely intense in peak season — the popular spots fill 60–90 minutes before sunset, and the narrow walkways become difficult to navigate in the half-hour before and after sunset itself. Quieter sunset experiences can be found from the upper terraces of Imerovigli, from the rim path between villages, and from the lesser-known viewpoints in Pyrgos.

Walking the caldera rim path from Fira to Oia is the island's signature hike. The 10-kilometre route follows the cliff edge through Firostefani, Imerovigli, and Skaros Rock before descending through the rural landscape between the villages and rising again to Oia. The walk takes 3–4 hours at moderate pace with stops for photographs and refreshment; the late-afternoon direction (Fira to Oia) puts the sun behind the walker and allows finishing in Oia in time for dinner. Early morning is significantly cooler and avoids the worst of the heat, with the trade-off of less dramatic light. Comfortable shoes are essential — the path includes sections of loose stone and significant elevation change.

Boat trips into the caldera and around the volcanic islands are an essential complement to the cliff-top views. The standard tour visits the still-active Nea Kameni volcanic island in the centre of the caldera (a 30-minute walk to the steaming crater rim), the hot springs at the smaller Palea Kameni (where the boat anchors and swimmers reach the sulphurous warm water), the village on Therasia island for lunch or coffee, and Oia for the famous sunset return. Pricing runs €40–80 for the standard group catamaran tour, €120–200 for higher-end small-group experiences, and €1,500–4,000 for fully private yacht charters. Sunset cruises are routinely the most popular booking and require advance reservation in season.

Wine tourism in the southern flatlands is genuinely excellent and underrated. The volcanic soil, the unique local Assyrtiko grape variety, and the traditional kouloura (basket-shaped) vine training method that protects the grapes from the wind have produced wines of distinctive character. The Santo Wines, Argyros, Estate Argyros, Domaine Sigalas, and Gaia Wines estates all offer tasting visits with views across the southern landscape; pricing runs €15–35 per person for small flights with food pairing. Wine tours covering 3–4 estates with transport run €80–150 per person. The Wine Museum at Volcan Wines in Vothonas offers a comprehensive introduction to the island's viticultural history.

The prehistoric site of Akrotiri at the southern end of the island represents one of the most important archaeological discoveries in the eastern Mediterranean. The Bronze Age city, abandoned and buried during the Minoan eruption around 1600 BCE, has been preserved by the volcanic ash including upper-floor walls, ceramic vessels, frescoes, and even furniture impressions. The site is covered by a modern roof structure that allows year-round visits regardless of weather. Entry costs €12 and the visit takes 1–1.5 hours; combining Akrotiri with the adjacent Red Beach makes a productive half-day in the southern part of the island.

Beaches on Santorini are entirely different in character from the typical Mediterranean white-sand image. The volcanic origin produces black sand at Perissa, Perivolos, Kamari, and Vlychada; red volcanic cliffs and red sand at Red Beach near Akrotiri; and the unusual White Beach accessible only by boat. The eastern beaches (Perissa, Perivolos, Kamari) are the most developed, with beach clubs offering sun loungers (€20–60 per day), restaurants, and a more relaxed atmosphere away from the caldera-rim crowds. Vlychada in the south has a more dramatic landscape with eroded cliffs in unusual shapes, and several upscale beach clubs.

The smaller villages of Pyrgos and Megalochori offer the best escape from the caldera-rim tourism. Pyrgos sits at the highest point of the island and was the historic capital before the modern shift to Fira; the medieval centre with its narrow lanes, small churches, and a Venetian-era kastro at the summit makes a rewarding 2-hour visit. Megalochori is a working village with traditional architecture, several wineries, and significantly better food-value at the local tavernas than the equivalent meal in Oia or Fira.

Hiking beyond the rim path includes the climb up Mount Profitis Ilias (567 metres), the highest point of the island, with panoramic views from the small monastery at the summit. The Skaros Rock promontory near Imerovigli — the ruins of a medieval fortress on a striking caldera-edge rock — offers a 30-minute walk down and back with some of the best caldera views on the island.

Day trips to neighbouring islands expand the experience. Anafi, 25 nautical miles east, is a quiet alternative Cyclades island reached by ferry in 1.5 hours. Folegandros, 60 kilometres northwest, has a similar caldera-rim village atmosphere on a much smaller scale and is reachable as a long day trip or 1–2 night stay. Crete, the largest Greek island, is reachable by ferry in 2 hours and supports multi-day extensions into a different scale of landscape.

Photography is itself a significant activity for many visitors, given the island's visual fame. The classic Oia sunset shot from the windmill viewpoint is essentially obligatory but routinely difficult to achieve given the crowds. Quieter alternatives include the blue-domed church viewpoints in Firostefani, the rooftop pools that become unintended Instagram backdrops at sunset, and the early-morning light on Pyrgos before the day-trip buses arrive. Drone restrictions are tight in the populated areas; checking current regulations and avoiding crowded viewpoints with drones is essential.
