# Santorini, Greece — Practical Information

Accommodation pricing on Santorini is among the highest in the Mediterranean and the price-quality relationship varies dramatically by location. Caldera-view accommodation in Oia, Imerovigli, and Firostefani commands a substantial premium for the view itself: budget rooms with caldera glimpses run €180–300 per night in shoulder season; mid-range cliffside hotels €350–700; the famous luxury cave suites with private plunge pools and full caldera views €800–3,000 in shoulder season and €1,500–5,000+ in peak summer. Equivalent quality rooms without the caldera view in inland villages or on the eastern coast cost 40–70% less. Hostel dorm beds and budget rooms in Fira run €40–90 per night. Eastern coast properties in Kamari and Perissa offer mid-range hotels at €100–250 per night with beach access. Peak season (mid-July through August) sees premiums of 30–60% above shoulder rates and requires booking 4–6 months ahead at the most popular caldera properties; the famous luxury cave suites routinely book out a year in advance for the prime sunset-view rooms.

Food costs are high in tourist areas and substantially more reasonable away from the caldera-rim villages. Casual lunch — gyros, souvlaki, salads — at a local taverna runs €12–20 per person. Mid-range dinner restaurants charge €30–50 per person for two courses with wine. Caldera-view restaurants in Oia and Imerovigli charge €60–120 per person for similar quality with the substantial view premium. Several Michelin-recommended restaurants (Selene at the moment of writing among others) charge €100–200 per person for tasting menus. Local Santorini specialties worth seeking out include fava (yellow split-pea purée), tomato fritters (tomatokeftedes), the local Assyrtiko white wine, and the Vinsanto sweet wine. Tipping is not expected in the way it is in North America; rounding up the bill or 10% for excellent service is the local convention.

Transport on Santorini centres on rental cars, ATVs, scooters, taxis, and the limited public bus network. Renting a car is the most practical mode for visitors planning to explore beyond a single base; daily rates run €30–80 in shoulder season and €60–150 in peak summer, with airport pickup available from all major international rental agencies. ATV and scooter rental ($30–60 per day) is widely popular but the narrow roads and tourist-heavy traffic produce a steady stream of accidents; visitors without prior experience should prefer cars. Parking in Oia and Fira is genuinely limited and expensive; many caldera hotels offer valet service that meaningfully simplifies the logistics.

The KTEL public bus network connects the main villages — Fira (the central hub), Oia, Kamari, Perissa, Akrotiri, Pyrgos — at €1.80–2.50 per ride; service is frequent in summer (every 30 minutes on the main routes) and substantially reduced in winter. Buses are the cheapest transport option and work well for visitors basing in Fira and exploring the major destinations. Taxis are available but limited in number — only around 35 licensed taxis serve the entire island — and can be difficult to find in peak season; pre-booking through accommodation is the standard approach. Uber does not operate on Santorini.

Santorini National Airport (JTR) handles direct flights from Athens (45 minutes), several European cities seasonally (London, Frankfurt, Amsterdam, Paris, Rome, Madrid, and others), and increasingly direct flights from select Middle Eastern hubs in summer. Domestic flights from Athens run year-round with frequent service; international direct flights operate primarily May through October. Ferry connections to Athens (Piraeus) take 5–8 hours by high-speed catamaran (€60–110) or 8–10 hours by conventional ferry (€40–70). Ferry connections to other Cycladic islands — Mykonos, Naxos, Paros, Ios — run frequently in summer, supporting island-hopping itineraries. The ferry port at Athinios is at the southwestern caldera edge and the climb to the rim villages is dramatic; pre-arranged transfers from accommodation cost €25–50.

Cards are widely accepted at hotels, mid-range and upscale restaurants, and tourist-oriented shops. Smaller tavernas, beach kiosks, and traditional shops are often cash-only or offer small discounts for cash payment. ATMs are available throughout the major villages; non-Greek-bank ATMs charge €5–10 in fees per withdrawal. The euro is the currency. EU visitors face no currency or border friction; non-EU visitors should plan for standard Schengen entry procedures.

Connectivity is good in populated areas and at all hotels; rural sections of the island and the more remote beaches have spotty coverage. EU roaming agreements mean European Union visitors use their domestic plans without surcharge. Non-EU visitors can buy Greek tourist SIMs from Cosmote, Vodafone, or Wind at the airport or in Fira; multi-week packages with substantial data run €15–30. Free WiFi is universal at hotels, restaurants, and most cafes.

The Meltemi wind, which blows from the north for stretches of summer (most intense in July and August), can disrupt ferry schedules, beach time, and small-boat caldera tours. Several days of strong winds within any given summer trip are normal; building scheduling flexibility around the highest-priority weather-dependent activities is sensible. Wind shadows on the eastern coast (Kamari, Perissa) provide more comfortable beach time during high-Meltemi periods than the exposed western and northern coasts.

Cruise ship days fundamentally change the character of the island. When multiple large cruise ships disembark 8,000–15,000 day visitors simultaneously (typically arriving 9–11 a.m. and departing 4–6 p.m.), the central villages — particularly Fira and Oia — become substantially more crowded. Cruise schedules are published publicly; checking arrival schedules and planning to be in less-affected parts of the island during cruise hours produces meaningfully better days.

Visa requirements for most Western nationalities are simple — Schengen Area entry for stays up to 90 days within any 180-day period for visa-exempt travellers. ETIAS authorization will become required for visa-exempt visitors during the 2025–2026 implementation; checking current requirements before booking is the practical approach.

Health considerations are generally minor. Tap water on Santorini is technically potable but most visitors and many locals prefer bottled water due to mineral content variability. Sun exposure at the southern Aegean latitude is intense, particularly given the white-painted surfaces' reflection; serious sun protection matters even on cloudy days. Medical care is available at the small Santorini hospital in Fira and through a network of private clinics; complex emergencies are airlifted to Athens.
