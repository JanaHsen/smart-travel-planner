# Barcelona, Spain — Things to Do

The Sagrada Família is the city's defining single attraction and Antoni Gaudí's enduring masterwork. Begun in 1882 and still under construction with completion targeted for 2026, the basilica's exterior alternates between Gothic-influenced stone tracery and the radically organic forms that defined Gaudí's mature style; the interior is a forest of branching stone columns supporting a vaulted ceiling that filters light through stained glass into shifting coloured patterns through the day. Entry is by timed ticket and advance booking is essential — same-day tickets are routinely unavailable in peak season. The basic ticket covers basilica entry; additional tickets allow tower access for elevated views over the surrounding city. Plan 1.5–2.5 hours for a thorough visit; afternoon late-light visits maximize the stained glass effects.

Park Güell, on a hill above the Gràcia district, is Gaudí's other landmark project — originally conceived as a planned residential development in the early 1900s, abandoned, and converted to public parkland. The Monumental Zone, containing the famous mosaic-tiled serpentine bench, the dragon staircase, and the colonnaded hall of the Sala Hipòstila, requires a timed-entry ticket; the surrounding park grounds are free and open. The walk up from the metro stations is steep and frequently difficult in summer heat; arriving by taxi or the dedicated bus from Plaça Catalunya is more comfortable. Sunrise visits avoid the worst of the crowds and produce the best photography conditions.

Other Gaudí works distributed through the Eixample include Casa Batlló (Passeig de Gràcia 43) and Casa Milà / La Pedrera (Passeig de Gràcia 92). Both are open as museums with audio guides; both routinely require advance booking. Casa Batlló's curved facade and dragon-spine roof are the most visually striking exterior; La Pedrera's rooftop with its surreal chimney sculptures is the more photographable interior experience. Visitors with limited time can choose one of these along with the Sagrada Família and Park Güell for a representative Gaudí circuit.

The Gothic Quarter (Barri Gòtic) is the medieval core of the city and rewards unstructured exploration over scheduled sightseeing. The Cathedral of Barcelona, distinct from the Sagrada Família, is the actual seat of the Archbishop and a serene Gothic structure with an unusual cloister housing thirteen white geese. Plaça Reial is the most photogenic square; Plaça del Pi and Plaça Sant Felip Neri are smaller, quieter, and worth seeking out. The neighbourhood is at its best in the early morning and late evening when the day-trip cruise crowds have departed.

El Born, immediately east of the Gothic Quarter, has emerged as the city's most fashionable district for dining, design boutiques, and small museums. The Picasso Museum on Carrer Montcada holds one of the world's most comprehensive collections of the artist's early work, including the famous late series of variations on Velázquez's Las Meninas. The Santa Maria del Mar church, completed in 1383, is the finest example of the Catalan Gothic style — austere, vertical, and acoustically remarkable.

Museum Montjuïc and the Joan Miró Foundation occupy the hill west of the historic centre. The Montjuïc complex includes the National Art Museum of Catalonia (MNAC), with the world's most important collection of Romanesque frescos rescued from Pyrenean churches in the early 20th century; the Olympic Stadium from 1992; the Magic Fountain with its summer evening light shows; and the Castle of Montjuïc, which hosted military fortifications and political prisons through Franco's regime. The Joan Miró Foundation, designed by Josep Lluís Sert, holds a deep collection of Miró's paintings, sculpture, and tapestries.

The Sant Antoni and Sant Pau neighbourhood markets, plus the famous La Boqueria off Las Ramblas, are working markets that double as essential cultural experiences. La Boqueria has become severely overcrowded with cruise tourists during peak hours; visiting at 8 a.m. or in the late afternoon produces a more authentic experience, and the Mercat de Sant Antoni is a less-touristed alternative. The cocktail and tapas culture of the city radiates from these markets; eating standing at a counter with a glass of vermouth and a few small dishes is the local rhythm.

The beach scene runs along the Mediterranean from Barceloneta in the centre to the developed beaches of Bogatell, Mar Bella, and Nova Mar Bella to the northeast. Barceloneta beach is the closest and busiest, packed in summer and surrounded by restaurants and bars. The further beaches are progressively less crowded and several have specific designations — Mar Bella has a clothing-optional section, Nova Mar Bella is dog-friendly, the smaller Sant Sebastià beach is favoured by locals. Beach restaurants (chiringuitos) operate seasonally; the seafood restaurants in Barceloneta proper are largely tourist-priced but a few survivors of the pre-tourism era still operate.

Day trips from Barcelona include Montserrat, the dramatic mountain monastery 60 kilometres northwest accessible by direct train and cable car; the medieval town of Girona, 100 kilometres north, with one of Europe's most preserved Jewish quarters; the wine country of Penedès, the home of cava and increasingly of fine still wines; and the coastal town of Sitges, 30 kilometres south, a popular weekend destination with a notable LGBTQ+ scene and excellent beaches. The Costa Brava beaches further north are accessible as long day trips but more rewarding as overnight stays.

The food and wine scene is among the strongest in Europe. The classic vermouth-and-tapas hour from 12 noon to 2 p.m. and the late-evening dinner culture (typically not before 9 p.m. for locals) define the local rhythm. Casual neighbourhood bars (bodegas, tabernas) offer some of the best food experiences at modest prices. Several Michelin-starred restaurants — Disfrutar, Cocina Hermanos Torres, and the legendary Tickets — represent the high end of contemporary Spanish dining; reservations require booking 1–3 months ahead.
