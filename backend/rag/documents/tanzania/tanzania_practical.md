# Tanzania — Practical Information

Safari pricing in Tanzania reflects the all-inclusive structure of the industry and is genuinely high by any global standard. Budget camping safaris with shared mess tents and basic camp facilities run $250–400 per person per day. Mid-range mobile tented camps with private bathrooms and good food cost $400–800 per person per day. Permanent lodges and high-end tented camps in premium locations within the parks run $800–1,500 per person per day. The ultra-luxury tier — properties like Singita Sasakwa, Singita Faru Faru, andBeyond Klein's Camp, the Four Seasons Serengeti, the small private conservancies — runs $1,500–3,500+ per person per day. These rates typically include accommodation, all meals, drinks, game drives in shared vehicles, park fees, conservation fees, and laundry; international flights, internal flights, gratuities, and premium experiences are additional. A 7-day northern circuit safari for two people commonly totals $4,000–15,000 plus international airfare.

Park entry fees and conservation fees represent a substantial fixed cost component. Serengeti National Park entry is $70 per adult per day; Ngorongoro Crater entry is $70 per adult per day plus a $300 vehicle entry fee per crater descent; smaller parks (Tarangire, Lake Manyara) charge $50–60 per day. The Ngorongoro Conservation Area Authority and Tanzania National Parks (TANAPA) fee structures change periodically; current rates should be confirmed with operators when booking. These fees are typically built into safari package pricing rather than charged separately.

Zanzibar accommodation pricing varies enormously by location and tier. Budget guesthouses in Stone Town and the more developed beach areas run $30–80 per night. Mid-range beachfront hotels and boutique properties in Stone Town cost $100–250 per night. High-end resorts on the east coast (Bawe, Kilindi, &Beyond Mnemba Island, Matemwe Lodge, the Aman) run $400–1,500+ per night. The Mnemba Island private island experience reaches $2,500+ per couple per night including meals, activities, and the boat transfers from the main island.

Mount Kilimanjaro climb pricing varies dramatically by operator and route length. The cheapest operators advertising sub-$1,500 climbs typically achieve those prices through inadequate porter wages, cheap and worn equipment, and substandard food and acclimatization protocols — the cost cuts produce both ethical concerns and reduced summit success rates. Reputable operators charging $2,500–4,000 per person for a 7-day Machame or 8-day Lemosho climb provide proper porter loads (under the 20kg KPAP guideline), quality high-altitude tents, experienced lead guides, full mountain catering, and the acclimatization profile that maximizes summit chances. Extra costs include tipping (mandatory in practice; $250–400 per climber distributed across the guide team), park fees ($800+ for the standard 7-day climb, included in operator pricing), and gear rental for items not brought from home ($100–300).

Transport between the major destinations involves a combination of internal flights, road transfers, and the boat connection to Zanzibar. Flights from Kilimanjaro International Airport (JRO) or Arusha Airport (ARK) to the safari airstrips run $200–400 per person each way. Coastal Aviation, Auric Air, Precision Air, and several smaller operators serve the Serengeti, Ngorongoro, and southern park airstrips. The flight from Arusha or Dar es Salaam to Zanzibar (1 hour) runs $80–250 each way depending on the carrier and booking lead time. The high-speed ferry from Dar es Salaam to Zanzibar (2 hours, $35–60 one way) is the alternative for visitors with the time. Road transfers between Arusha and the northern parks are included in safari package pricing; the drives are long (4–8 hours to most parks) and most visitors fly in to maximize game-drive time.

International arrivals concentrate at three airports. Kilimanjaro International Airport (JRO) is the standard entry point for northern circuit safaris and Kilimanjaro climbs, with direct flights from Amsterdam, Doha, Istanbul, Addis Ababa, and Dar es Salaam. Julius Nyerere International Airport in Dar es Salaam (DAR) handles the broader range of international flights and serves as the gateway to the southern parks and the ferry to Zanzibar. Zanzibar International Airport (ZNZ) accepts direct international flights from Doha, Istanbul, several European charter operators, and regional African connections.

Cards are accepted at upscale hotels, established restaurants in cities, and major safari operators; everywhere else is cash-based. Visa and Mastercard work at the major properties; American Express acceptance is limited. ATMs in Arusha, Moshi, Dar es Salaam, and Zanzibar Stone Town reliably accept foreign cards; withdrawal limits run 400,000–800,000 TZS ($150–300) per transaction with foreign-card fees of 10,000–20,000 TZS. The Tanzanian shilling is the local currency but US dollars are universally accepted in tourist contexts; many tourist services price exclusively in dollars. Crisp post-2009 US bills in good condition are essential — older or worn bills are commonly rejected.

Connectivity is good in the cities and major safari hubs and patchy or absent in the parks themselves. Tourist SIMs from Vodacom, Tigo, or Airtel cost 20,000–50,000 TZS ($8–20) for multi-week packages with substantial data; coverage from Vodacom is generally the most extensive. WiFi is standard at hotels in Arusha, Moshi, Dar es Salaam, and Zanzibar; in-park lodge WiFi (where available) is usually limited to common areas via satellite at restricted speeds. The disconnection during safari days is widely considered part of the experience.

Health considerations for Tanzania are more substantial than for most destinations. Yellow fever vaccination is required for travel from countries with risk of yellow fever transmission and is checked at entry; many visitors get the vaccine prophylactically. Malaria prophylaxis is recommended for all areas including Zanzibar and the safari parks except the immediate Mount Kilimanjaro climbing area; the standard options are atovaquone-proguanil (Malarone), doxycycline, or mefloquine, with prescriptions to be obtained before departure. Routine vaccines (Hepatitis A, typhoid, tetanus) are advised. Tap water is not potable; bottled water is universal at lodges and camps. The standard travel-stomach precautions apply at lower-end establishments.

Altitude considerations apply specifically to Kilimanjaro climbs and to the Ngorongoro Crater rim. The crater rim sits at 2,300 metres and visitors flying directly from sea level may experience mild altitude effects on the first day. Kilimanjaro summit at 5,895 metres is genuinely high altitude with serious risk of altitude sickness; the summit success rate correlates strongly with the route length chosen and the acclimatization profile, with 8+ day routes substantially safer than 5–6 day routes.

Safety considerations for safari and beach tourism are generally moderate. The major safari operations are highly professional and the wildlife encounter risks are managed effectively through experienced guides and standardized vehicle protocols. Petty theft in the cities (particularly in central Dar es Salaam and parts of Arusha) is at meaningful levels; standard urban-travel precautions apply. Zanzibar Stone Town is generally safe during the day and through the early evening; late-night solo walking in the narrow alleys is best avoided. The mainland coast away from the major tourist areas presents specific risks (poor road infrastructure, limited medical access) that organized tour operators manage but that independent travel choices should account for.

Visa requirements for most Western nationalities are straightforward — single-entry tourist visas at $50 (most nationalities) or $100 (US citizens) for stays up to 90 days, available on arrival at Kilimanjaro, Dar es Salaam, and Zanzibar airports. The e-Visa system at the Tanzania Immigration website allows pre-arrival application with the same fee structure. Both visa-on-arrival and e-Visa work reliably; pre-arrival application avoids the airport queue.

Tipping in Tanzania is more structured than in most destinations and represents a meaningful component of staff income. Safari guides typically receive $20–30 per day per couple from the guests; safari camp staff (collectively) receive $10–20 per day per couple. Mountain guide tips are higher and structured by role — typically $200–250 for the lead guide, $150–200 for the assistant guide, $20–25 per porter, and $30–50 for the cook on a standard 7-day Kilimanjaro climb, totalling $250–400 per climber distributed across the team. These figures represent floors rather than ceilings; exceptional service is appropriately recognized with higher amounts.
