# Tanzania — Things to Do

Game viewing on the northern safari circuit is the country's headline activity and the experience that drives the bulk of international visits. The Serengeti National Park, covering 14,750 square kilometres, supports the highest density of grazing herbivores on earth and the predator populations that depend on them. Game drives in open-sided 4x4 safari vehicles produce reliable sightings of all the famous savanna wildlife — lions in family prides, leopards in trees, cheetahs hunting on the open plains, elephants in matriarch-led herds, giraffes browsing the acacia, and the wildebeest and zebra of the migration in the millions during the right seasons. The Ngorongoro Crater, a 260-square-kilometre caldera that is the largest unbroken volcanic caldera in the world, contains one of the densest large-mammal populations anywhere, including the most reliable place in northern Tanzania to see the critically endangered black rhino. Lake Manyara National Park, smaller and quieter, is famous for its tree-climbing lions and large flamingo populations on the alkaline lake. Tarangire National Park, particularly during the dry season, holds elephant herds among the iconic baobab trees and offers excellent game viewing without the Serengeti crowds.

The Great Migration is the country's defining wildlife spectacle and the timing significantly shapes any wildlife-focused itinerary. The annual cycle moves the herds — approximately 1.5 million wildebeest, 200,000 zebra, and accompanying gazelle, predators, and scavengers — through a roughly clockwise circuit covering the Serengeti and the Maasai Mara reserves in Kenya across the calendar year. Calving season runs roughly January–March in the southern Serengeti's short-grass plains; the herds move north through April–June, reach the western corridor and Grumeti River crossings in late June and July, and reach the Mara River crossings between the northern Serengeti and Kenya's Maasai Mara in late July through October. The river crossings — vast herds plunging across crocodile-infested water — are the most dramatic wildlife spectacle on earth and the priority experience for many visitors planning around the migration. November brings the herds back south through the eastern Serengeti as the short rains green up the southern plains.

Mount Kilimanjaro climbs are the country's marquee adventure activity and one of the most accessible high-altitude climbs on a major peak in the world. The mountain has multiple route options — Marangu (5–6 days, the only route with hut accommodation), Machame (6–7 days, the most popular and beautiful), Lemosho (7–9 days, the highest summit success rate), Rongai (6–7 days, the only route from the dry northern side), and Northern Circuit (8–9 days, the longest and least crowded). All climbs must be done with licensed operators who provide guides, porters, cooks, and equipment. Pricing varies dramatically by operator quality and route length: budget operators run $1,500–2,000 per person, mid-range $2,000–3,500, premium operators with the highest porter standards and equipment quality $3,500–6,000+. The summit success rate varies by route from around 65% (5-day Marangu) to over 90% (8-day Lemosho); longer routes allow better acclimatization and substantially better odds of reaching Uhuru Peak. Physical preparation is essential but no technical mountaineering skills are required for the standard routes.

Zanzibar is the standard counterpoint to the safari experience and the bulk of safari visitors include 3–7 days on the islands at the end of their trip. Stone Town, the historic centre of Zanzibar Town and a UNESCO World Heritage site, contains the dense layered architecture of the Swahili coast — coral-stone buildings with elaborately carved wooden doors, narrow alleys winding between former merchant houses, and the still-active markets of the spice trade. The House of Wonders and the Old Fort provide context for the layered Omani, Portuguese, German, and British colonial periods. The infamous slave market site, with its underground holding chambers and the Anglican cathedral built directly over the market, remains one of the most affecting historical sites on the continent. Spice farm tours in the rural interior offer an introduction to the cloves, nutmeg, cinnamon, and pepper plantations that drove the island's historic economy.

Zanzibar's beaches divide into east coast and northeast coast (Paje, Jambiani, Bwejuu, and the famous Matemwe and Pongwe), the northwest tip (Nungwi and Kendwa), the southwest coast (Kizimkazi for dolphin tours), and the smaller islands offshore (Mnemba Island for high-end snorkeling and diving). The east coast has the most extensive resort development and the famous tide-dependent beach experiences; the northwest beaches face west and have the most reliable swimming conditions throughout the day; the southwestern coast offers the best dolphin and snorkeling experiences. Kite-surfing has emerged as a major draw at Paje, with consistent winds and warm shallow water producing excellent learning conditions; multiple schools offer instruction.

The southern safari circuit — Ruaha National Park, Nyerere National Park (formerly Selous), and Mikumi — offers a fundamentally different safari experience from the famous northern parks. Visitor numbers are a small fraction of the Serengeti's; vehicle traffic at sightings is minimal; the wildlife (lions, elephants, leopards, wild dogs, hippos, crocodiles in vast numbers) is comparable to the north. Boat safaris on the Rufiji River in Nyerere offer water-based wildlife viewing impossible in the dry northern parks. Walking safaris with armed rangers are more widely available in the southern parks. The trade-off is access — most southern park visits require fly-in itineraries from Dar es Salaam or Arusha, with limited road access from the major centres.

Chimpanzee tracking in the western parks is one of Africa's most exclusive wildlife experiences. Mahale Mountains National Park and Gombe Stream National Park, both on the eastern shore of Lake Tanganyika, protect the habituated chimpanzee populations made famous by Jane Goodall's decades of research. Daily treks led by researcher-trained guides find the chimpanzee groups in the forest and allow up to one hour of close observation. The logistics — flights from Dar es Salaam to small airstrips, boat transfers along the lake, and accommodation at one of the small handful of lodges — produce an exclusivity that comes with proportionate pricing ($1,200–2,500 per person per day). The experience itself, watching habituated wild chimpanzees in their natural habitat from metres away, is among the most memorable wildlife encounters available anywhere.

Cultural visits to Maasai villages near the safari circuits offer an introduction to one of East Africa's most distinctive pastoral cultures. The standard tourist visits are inevitably commercialized but well-organized programs through the major safari operators include genuine engagement with daily life, livestock economy, and traditional celebrations. The Hadzabe and Datoga ethnic groups in the Lake Eyasi region offer more remote cultural experiences; visits to Hadzabe hunting groups (one of the last remaining hunter-gatherer societies in Africa) are arranged through specific operators based in Karatu or Mto wa Mbu.

Beach activities from the Zanzibar coast extend well beyond traditional resort experiences. Dhow sailing in traditional wooden boats, sunset cruises with snorkeling, deep-sea fishing for marlin and sailfish, and PADI scuba certification courses are all widely available. The reefs around Mnemba Atoll and the Pemba Channel offer excellent diving with high biodiversity and clear water; pricing is comparable to other Indian Ocean dive destinations.
