# Tanzania — Overview

Tanzania occupies a substantial area of East Africa, bordered by Kenya and Uganda to the north, Rwanda, Burundi, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo to the west, Zambia, Malawi, and Mozambique to the south, and the Indian Ocean to the east. The country covers roughly 945,000 square kilometres — about twice the size of California — and supports a population of around 65 million people. Within its borders sit some of Africa's most iconic natural features: Mount Kilimanjaro (5,895 metres, the highest peak on the continent), the Serengeti ecosystem hosting the world's largest land migration, the Ngorongoro Crater holding one of the densest concentrations of large mammals on earth, and the Indian Ocean archipelago of Zanzibar with its layered Swahili, Arab, Persian, and Indian cultural heritage.

The country was formed by the 1964 union of mainland Tanganyika (which gained independence from Britain in 1961) and the offshore Zanzibar archipelago (independent from Britain in 1963). The portmanteau name combines the two. The first president, Julius Nyerere, established a one-party socialist state and pursued a distinctive ideology of African socialism (ujamaa) based on collective village agriculture. The economic experiments largely failed but the political stability and the deliberate de-emphasis of ethnic identity produced a country that has remained free of the ethnic conflict that has destabilized much of the region.

Tanzania divides geographically into several distinct zones. The northern safari circuit — Serengeti, Ngorongoro, Lake Manyara, Tarangire, Arusha — concentrates the country's most famous wildlife destinations within a 200-kilometre arc. The southern parks — Ruaha, Selous (now Nyerere), and Mikumi — offer comparable wildlife with significantly lower visitor numbers and a more remote-Africa atmosphere. The western parks — Mahale and Gombe — protect the chimpanzee habitats made famous by Jane Goodall's research and require dedicated multi-day flights and boat journeys. Mount Kilimanjaro and the surrounding Kilimanjaro National Park sit in the far north near the Kenyan border. The Indian Ocean coast holds Dar es Salaam (the largest city, though not the capital), the historic ports of Bagamoyo and Kilwa, and the Zanzibar archipelago.

The climate varies significantly by region and elevation. The coast and Zanzibar are hot and humid year-round (24–32°C) with two distinct rainy seasons — the long rains from March to May, and the short rains from October to December. The northern safari circuit at 1,000–2,000 metres elevation is cooler year-round (15–28°C) with the same two rainy patterns. Mount Kilimanjaro creates its own climate zones from tropical at the base through alpine desert to glacial at the summit. The southern parks are hot and dry June through October and progressively wetter from November.

Best time to visit depends entirely on the activities planned. For the Great Migration in the Serengeti — wildebeest and zebra herds moving in vast aggregations through the ecosystem — the timing depends on which phase of the migration is the priority: calving season in the southern Serengeti runs January–March; the river crossings of the Mara River in northern Serengeti peak July–September. For general game viewing in the northern circuit, the dry season of June–October offers the best wildlife viewing as animals congregate at remaining water sources, with clear weather and minimal mosquito activity; the trade-off is high-season pricing and busier camps. For climbing Kilimanjaro, the dry seasons of January–March and June–October are the standard windows. For Zanzibar, the dry season from June to October offers the best beach weather, though the November short rains and the calmer seas of December–February also work well.

Tanzania safari pricing is high by global standards and the cost structure differs significantly from most other tourism destinations. The standard package includes accommodation, meals, drinks, park entry fees ($60–80 per person per day), conservation fees, vehicle hire, and a guide; this all-inclusive structure produces daily costs of $250–400 per person at budget-camping safaris, $400–800 at mid-range lodges and tented camps, $800–1,500 at high-end lodges, and $1,500–3,500+ at the small group of ultra-luxury properties (Singita, andBeyond, Beyond's Sasakwa, and similar). A 7-day northern-circuit safari for two people commonly runs $4,000–15,000 all-in depending on the accommodation tier. Self-drive options exist but are uncommon for first-time visitors given the navigation, mechanical, and wildlife-encounter challenges.

Swahili (Kiswahili) is the national language and the lingua franca across East Africa; English is widely spoken in tourist-facing businesses and in the cities. A handful of Swahili greetings — jambo (hello), asante (thank you), karibu (welcome) — are universally appreciated. Outside the safari circuits and tourist centres, English knowledge drops substantially.

The country's combination of safari, climbing, and beach destinations supports both single-purpose visits (a 7-day safari, a Kilimanjaro climb, a Zanzibar beach week) and combined itineraries (the classic safari-and-Zanzibar two-week trip is extremely popular). Internal flights via Coastal Aviation, Auric Air, Precision Air, and Air Tanzania connect the safari airstrips, Zanzibar, and the major cities, allowing efficient itinerary combinations that would be impractical by road.
