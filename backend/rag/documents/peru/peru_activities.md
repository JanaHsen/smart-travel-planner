# Peru — Things to Do

Machu Picchu is the country's defining single attraction and the destination that drives the bulk of Peru's tourism. The Inca citadel sits at 2,430 metres on a ridge above the Urubamba River, surrounded by densely forested peaks; the site was abandoned during the Spanish conquest, escaped colonial detection, and was reintroduced to the wider world by Hiram Bingham's 1911 expedition. The site is reached either by train from Cusco or the Sacred Valley to Aguas Calientes (the small town in the valley below), or by trekking — most famously via the 4-day Inca Trail. Daily visitor caps strictly limit access to the site itself; entry tickets are tied to specific time slots and circuits within the complex, and must be booked in advance. The peak summer months (June–August) see tickets selling out 1–3 months ahead; shoulder season planning is more flexible. Machu Picchu Mountain (additional 3,082-metre summit hike from the citadel) and Huayna Picchu (the dramatic peak in the famous photographs) both require separate tickets and additional climbs of 1.5–2 hours.

The Inca Trail is the most famous trekking route to Machu Picchu but is one of several alternatives. The classic 4-day, 43-kilometre route requires booking 4–6 months in advance through licensed operators; the trail is permit-restricted to 500 people per day including guides and porters, and books out years in advance for peak season dates. The Salkantay Trek (5 days, no permit required) circles around the Salkantay massif with comparable scenery and is the standard alternative. The Lares Trek (3–4 days) emphasizes Andean village culture over archaeology. The Choquequirao trek (4–9 days) accesses a remote Inca site equally significant as Machu Picchu but with a fraction of the visitor numbers. All multi-day treks require physical fitness, advance booking, and acclimatization to the altitudes (4,200+ metres at the highest passes).

The Sacred Valley between Cusco and Machu Picchu rewards 2–4 days of dedicated exploration. Pisac, with its dramatic terraced ruins and large craft market, sits at the eastern end of the valley. Ollantayambo, an active village built on Inca foundations, has the best-preserved Inca street grid and a dramatic terraced fortress climbing the hill above town. Maras and Moray, both reached from the upper valley, contain salt evaporation ponds that have been worked since pre-Inca times and a series of concentric circular agricultural terraces of unknown precise function. Chinchero, a high-altitude village of weavers, includes one of the best places to learn about traditional Andean textile production.

Cusco itself is rich enough to justify several days. The city's central plaza is ringed by the Cathedral of Cusco (built on the foundation of the Inca palace of Inca Wiracocha) and the Iglesia de la Compañía. The Coricancha temple, the most sacred building in the Inca empire, was partially demolished and the Santo Domingo convent built on its foundations; the surviving Inca masonry remains visible. The walls of Sacsayhuamán, the massive ceremonial complex above the city, contain stones weighing up to 200 tons fitted together with the precision that became the Inca architectural signature. The San Pedro market offers some of the best food experience in the country at unbeatable prices. The San Blas neighbourhood, the artistic quarter, contains the best small craft galleries and the most interesting lesser churches.

Lima rewards a dedicated 2–3 day stay despite many visitors treating it as a layover. The Miraflores and Barranco neighbourhoods on the Pacific cliffs hold the best dining, the contemporary art scene, and the most pleasant urban walks. The historic Centro Histórico contains Spanish colonial architecture comparable to anywhere in the Americas, including the cathedral and the Convento de San Francisco with its catacombs. The Larco Museum, in a converted 18th-century mansion, holds one of the world's best pre-Columbian art collections including the famous and unusual erotic ceramics gallery. Lima's gastronomy scene runs from the famous Central and Maido (both regularly in the world's top 10 lists) through dozens of excellent mid-tier restaurants serving criolla, nikkei (Japanese-Peruvian), chifa (Chinese-Peruvian), and contemporary fusion cuisine.

Lake Titicaca, on the Bolivian border at 3,800 metres, is the highest navigable lake in the world by surface area and the centre of the Aymara culture. The town of Puno serves as the standard base; visits typically include a boat trip to the Uros people's floating reed islands (theatricalized but still genuinely interesting) and to Taquile and Amantani islands, which retain traditional pre-conquest social structures and offer overnight homestays. The complete tour requires 2 nights minimum.

Arequipa, the country's second city at 2,335 metres in the southern Andes, offers a striking contrast to Cusco — cleaner, less touristy, and architecturally distinct with its volcanic-stone colonial buildings. The Santa Catalina Monastery is a city within the city, a 16th-century convent that operated nearly independently for 300 years and contains one of the most atmospheric historic complexes in South America. The Colca Canyon, 2–3 hours from Arequipa, is among the world's deepest canyons and one of the most reliable places to observe Andean condors at the cross-canyon viewpoints. Multi-day trekking into the canyon is increasingly popular.

The Amazon offers a fundamentally different experience from the rest of the country. Iquitos in the north, accessible only by air or river boat, is the gateway to the Pacaya-Samiria reserve and the broadest range of jungle lodge experiences. Puerto Maldonado in the south offers easier access from Cusco (a 30-minute flight) and serves the Tambopata National Reserve, including the famous Tambopata Macaw Clay Lick. Multi-day jungle lodge stays (3–5 nights) include guided wildlife observation walks, river trips, canopy walks, fishing, and night excursions. Sightings include monkeys, caimans, river dolphins, sloths, dozens of bird species; the larger predators (jaguars, anacondas) are rare but possible.

The Nazca Lines on the southern coast are best appreciated from the small aircraft that operate flights over the geoglyph-covered desert; the famous figures (the spider, monkey, hummingbird, condor, and others) are clearly visible from the air and largely incomprehensible from ground level. Combining the Nazca flights with the desert oasis of Huacachina (sandboarding and dune buggy rides) and the Paracas national reserve makes a 2–3 day southern coast circuit.

The Cordillera Blanca trekking region around Huaraz, north of Lima, is South America's premier high-mountain trekking destination outside of Patagonia. The Santa Cruz Trek, the Huayhuash Circuit, and dozens of shorter day hikes access glaciated peaks above 6,000 metres, turquoise glacial lakes, and high-altitude valleys. The region is significantly less visited than Cusco and offers some of the country's most dramatic landscapes; visitors planning these treks should allow 8–14 days for proper acclimatization and the trek itself.
