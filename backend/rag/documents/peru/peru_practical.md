# Peru — Practical Information

Accommodation pricing in Peru spans a wide range with significant regional variation. Hostel dorm beds run 35–80 PEN ($9–22) per night across most of the country, with Cusco and Lima at the higher end. Mid-range hotels and guesthouses in Cusco, the Sacred Valley, Lima, and Arequipa run 200–500 PEN ($55–135) per night for a double room. Boutique hotels in well-located historic buildings cost 500–1,200 PEN ($135–325). The high-end tier — Inkaterra properties, the Belmond Hotel Monasterio in Cusco, the Belmond Hiram Bingham luxury train — runs 1,500–6,000+ PEN ($400–1,600+) per night. The famous Belmond Sanctuary Lodge at Machu Picchu (the only hotel actually adjacent to the citadel) runs $1,500–3,500 per night for one of the few rooms at this exclusive location. Peak season (June–August) sees prices roughly 30–50% above shoulder rates and requires booking 2–4 months ahead at popular properties.

Food costs are notably reasonable. Set-menu lunches at neighbourhood restaurants — known as menú or menú del día — run 12–25 PEN ($3–7) per person and represent the best food value in the country. Casual dinner restaurants charge 35–80 PEN ($9–22) per main course. Mid-range restaurants in Lima, Cusco, and Arequipa run 60–150 PEN per main course. The famous Lima fine dining scene is its own pricing tier — tasting menus at Central, Maido, Mil, and the small group of similarly-positioned restaurants run 800–1,800 PEN ($215–485) per person, requiring booking 2–6 months in advance. Pisco sour, the national cocktail, runs 20–50 PEN at most restaurants. Tipping is appreciated but more modest than in North America; 10% at sit-down restaurants is the standard convention.

Transport between regions involves a mix of flights, trains, and buses. Internal flights connect Lima to Cusco (1.5 hours, $80–250 one way), Arequipa (1.5 hours), Iquitos (2 hours), Puerto Maldonado (1.5 hours via Cusco), Trujillo, and Chiclayo. LATAM, Sky Airline, and JetSMART are the main carriers; pricing is volatile and last-minute fares can be 2–3 times advance-booked rates. The train from Cusco or Ollantaytambo to Aguas Calientes for Machu Picchu is operated by PeruRail and Inca Rail, with multiple service tiers from the budget-friendly Expedition (around $70 round trip) through the Vistadome with panoramic windows and the luxury Hiram Bingham (around $1,000 round trip including meals and entry to the citadel). Peru's long-distance bus network is extensive and notably comfortable — the Cruz del Sur and Oltursa premium services offer reclining seats, meals, and onboard service that exceed many countries' first-class trains; long routes like Lima-Arequipa (16 hours) or Lima-Cusco (22 hours) are popular among budget travellers.

Within cities, transport options vary by location. Lima has a limited modern metro system, the Metropolitano BRT bus line, taxis (negotiate before getting in or use the meter), and Uber/Cabify ride-hailing apps that work reliably. Cusco is small enough that the historic centre is fully walkable; taxis and Uber handle longer trips at modest cost (5–15 PEN within the city). The Sacred Valley is best explored by hired driver, group tour, or rental car; public colectivo minibuses connect the major valley towns at minimal cost.

Lima's Jorge Chávez International Airport (LIM) handles essentially all international arrivals, with direct flights from major North American hubs (Miami, Los Angeles, Houston, New York, Atlanta), several European cities (Madrid, Amsterdam, Paris, Frankfurt), and regional connections throughout Latin America. The airport is in the Callao district, 30–60 minutes from Miraflores depending on traffic; pre-arranged transfers run 80–150 PEN, taxis from authorized stands 60–100 PEN. The new Jorge Chávez terminal opened in 2025 with substantially improved facilities.

Cards are accepted at hotels, mid-range and upscale restaurants, and tourist-oriented shops; Visa and Mastercard are universal, American Express less so. Smaller restaurants, market stalls, taxis, and most rural businesses are cash-only. ATMs are widely available in cities and major tourist destinations; foreign card fees of 15–25 PEN per withdrawal apply at most ATMs. The Peruvian sol (PEN) is the currency; US dollars are also widely accepted in tourist areas, though usually at slightly unfavourable conversion rates compared to using soles directly. Exchange dollars at established casas de cambio in larger cities for the best rates.

Connectivity has improved substantially. Tourist SIMs from Claro, Movistar, or Bitel cost 30–80 PEN for multi-week packages with substantial data; Claro generally has the most extensive coverage. WiFi is standard at hotels, restaurants, and the major coworking spaces in Lima's Miraflores and Barranco. Mobile data and WiFi coverage in the highlands are reliable in towns and patchy in rural valleys; Amazon lodge connectivity is generally limited to common areas via satellite.

Altitude considerations deserve specific attention. Cusco sits at 3,400 metres and many visitors fly directly from Lima (sea level), producing acute altitude exposure. Common symptoms include headaches, fatigue, mild nausea, and shortness of breath; most visitors adapt within 24–48 hours but some experience more serious symptoms. Spending the first 1–2 days at lower altitude in the Sacred Valley (2,800 metres) before ascending to Cusco is the most effective acclimatization strategy. Acetazolamide (Diamox) prescribed before travel reduces symptoms for many travellers; the traditional coca leaf tea, available everywhere in the highlands, has milder but real effects. Visitors with cardiovascular conditions, severe hypertension, or pulmonary disease should consult their doctors before high-altitude travel.

Health considerations beyond altitude are moderate. Yellow fever vaccination is required for travel to the Amazon regions and recommended for travel to areas above 2,300 metres in the Cordillera; routine vaccines (Hepatitis A, typhoid, tetanus) are advised. Mosquito-borne illnesses including dengue and Zika are present in the Amazon and along the coast; standard repellent precautions apply. Tap water is not potable anywhere in the country; bottled water is universal. Food hygiene at established restaurants is good; street food and markets vary, with the standard travel-stomach precautions applying.

Safety considerations are moderate. Lima has experienced rising street crime in some neighbourhoods over the past several years; tourist areas (Miraflores, Barranco, San Isidro, the historic centre during daylight) remain reasonably safe but standard precautions apply. Cusco and the major tourist destinations of the highlands are generally safe with petty theft as the main risk. The Amazon presents specific risks — wildlife encounters, river safety, and remote-area medical access — that responsible lodge operators manage effectively. Solo female travel is generally manageable with standard precautions; the country has a moderate-to-high catcalling culture in many areas.

Visa requirements for most Western nationalities are straightforward — 90 to 183 days of visa-free entry on arrival depending on nationality, with U.S., Canadian, U.K., E.U., and Australian passports receiving the longer permit. Border officials may request proof of onward travel and sufficient funds. The Andean Migration Card (TAM) is now electronic and integrated with the entry stamp.

Entry fees and tickets for major attractions require advance planning. Machu Picchu entry tickets must be purchased through the official government website and are tied to specific time slots and circuits; same-day tickets are essentially never available. Boleto Turístico passes covering multiple sites in Cusco and the Sacred Valley represent significant savings over individual entries and are available in 1, 2, or 10-day formats.
