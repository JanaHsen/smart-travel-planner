# Peru — Overview

Peru occupies the central western edge of South America, with a Pacific coastline running 2,400 kilometres north-to-south, the high Andes mountains forming the country's spine, and the Amazon basin covering its eastern half. The country's geographic diversity is among the most extreme on the planet — coastal desert, snow-capped peaks above 6,000 metres, cloud forest, and dense tropical jungle all exist within Peru's borders, sometimes within a few hundred kilometres of each other. The population of 34 million is concentrated heavily in Lima (10 million in the metropolitan area) and along the coast, with substantial indigenous Quechua and Aymara populations in the highlands and smaller indigenous groups throughout the Amazon.

Peru was the heart of the Inca Empire, the largest pre-Columbian civilization in the Americas, and earlier civilizations including the Moche, Nazca, Wari, and Chimú left their own monumental archaeological traces. The Spanish conquest beginning in 1532 imposed colonial structures over the indigenous societies, and the resulting cultural blend defines much of contemporary Peru — the architecture of Cusco overlaying Inca foundations with Spanish colonial walls, the religious festivals combining Catholic processions with Andean celestial cosmology, the cuisine merging indigenous ingredients with Spanish, African, Chinese, and Japanese influences brought by successive waves of immigration.

The country divides into three principal regions for travel planning. The coast (la costa) contains Lima, the major colonial cities of Trujillo and Arequipa (the "white city" built from white volcanic stone), and the desert oases of Huacachina; the climate is arid year-round with cool grey winters and warm summers. The Andean highlands (la sierra) contain Cusco, the Sacred Valley, Machu Picchu, Lake Titicaca, and the Cordillera Blanca trekking region; the climate is divided into a dry season (May–October) and wet season (November–April), with temperatures dropping sharply at night year-round. The Amazon (la selva) covers the eastern lowlands with rainforest accessed primarily through Iquitos in the north and Puerto Maldonado in the south; the climate is hot and humid year-round with heavy rainfall, particularly December through March.

Best time to visit depends entirely on the regions in the itinerary. For the highlands — by far the most common visitor focus given Machu Picchu's draw — May through October is the dry season with reliable weather, clear skies, and the conditions necessary for the Inca Trail trek. June through August is peak season with the best weather and the highest prices and crowds. April, May, September, and October are excellent shoulder months. November through April brings rainy-season conditions in the highlands; the Inca Trail closes in February for maintenance. The coast is best visited December through April when the famous overcast Lima winter (May–November) gives way to sunshine; the Amazon is technically year-round but has marginally better access conditions in the May–October dry-ish months.

Costs in Peru are reasonable compared to Western countries but vary significantly by region. Daily costs of $50–100 cover comfortable mid-range accommodation, three meals, transport, and activities in most parts of the country. Budget travellers can manage on $30–45 per day; luxury options including the famous Belmond properties (the Hiram Bingham luxury train, the Hotel Monasterio in Cusco, the Belmond Sanctuary Lodge at Machu Picchu) run from $400 to several thousand per night. Lima has the most expensive restaurant scene and is home to two of the world's top 50 restaurants (Central and Maido), with tasting menus running $200–400 per person.

The country's cultural and culinary depth has positioned it as one of South America's most rewarding destinations for both first-time and repeat visitors. Lima's gastronomy scene, the archaeological richness of the Sacred Valley and Cusco, the high-altitude landscapes around Lake Titicaca, the colonial architecture of Arequipa, and the Amazon biodiversity each justify dedicated visit time. A first visit of 10–14 days typically focuses on Lima, Cusco, the Sacred Valley, Machu Picchu, and one additional region; comprehensive coverage of the country's highlights requires 3 weeks or more.

Spanish is the dominant language; Quechua remains widely spoken in the highlands and is officially recognized. English is spoken in tourist-facing businesses but with substantially less penetration than in many other major destinations; basic Spanish meaningfully improves the experience throughout the country.

Altitude is the single most consequential health planning factor. Cusco sits at 3,400 metres; Lake Titicaca at 3,800 metres; the Sacred Valley at 2,800 metres; and several common trekking destinations exceed 4,500 metres. Altitude sickness affects roughly half of visitors arriving directly at high altitude from sea level, with symptoms ranging from mild headaches to serious medical emergencies. Most visitors plan a day or two of acclimatization in Cusco before more demanding altitude exposure; pharmacological prophylaxis (acetazolamide) and the traditional coca leaf tea both help.
