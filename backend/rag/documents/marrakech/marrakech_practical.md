# Marrakech, Morocco — Practical Information

Accommodation in Marrakech spans an exceptional range of prices and styles, with the riad — a traditional courtyard house converted to a guesthouse — being the distinctive local format. Budget riads with private rooms run 250–500 dirham (approximately $25–50) per night, often including breakfast. Mid-range riads with rooftop terraces, plunge pools, and well-restored architectural detail cost 700–1,500 dirham ($70–150) per night. High-end boutique riads in restored palaces run 2,000–5,000 dirham ($200–500) per night. The famous luxury palace hotels — La Mamounia, Royal Mansour, the Selman — start at 5,000 dirham and run well above 20,000 dirham ($2,000+) for premium suites. Hostels with dorm beds (180–350 dirham) exist but the riad infrastructure makes private rooms genuinely affordable for budget-conscious travellers.

Food costs are reasonable across all categories. A tagine and bread with mint tea at a casual restaurant runs 60–120 dirham ($6–12) per person. Mid-range restaurants charge 150–350 dirham per person for a full meal with wine. The famous food stalls of Jemaa el-Fnaa offer dinner for 50–100 dirham, though the experience and the stalls' reliability for visitors are mixed — established stalls with consistent local clientele are safer choices than the most aggressive touts. Upscale restaurants in the medina and the Palmeraie charge 600–1,500 dirham per person for tasting menus with wine. Mint tea — universal, ceremonial, sweet — is offered constantly and rarely costs more than 15–25 dirham at a cafe.

Transport options vary in quality and value. Petit taxis (small red taxis, typically Dacia Logans) are the standard urban transport. They are metered in theory but the meter is rarely used for tourists; agreeing on a price before getting in (typically 20–60 dirham within the city) is the practical approach. Grand taxis (older Mercedes sedans, painted beige) handle longer distances and shared inter-city routes; they take six passengers and operate on fixed routes. Calèches — horse-drawn carriages — operate as tourist transport from the main squares and are charming but not particularly efficient. The municipal bus network exists but is rarely useful for visitor itineraries. Walking is by far the most practical way to navigate the medina; cars cannot enter most of it.

Drivers should think carefully before renting a car for a Marrakech-only visit; the medina is impenetrable to cars and parking elsewhere is challenging. For multi-day trips into the Atlas Mountains, the desert, or other Moroccan cities, hiring a car with a driver is standard and surprisingly affordable — 600–1,200 dirham per day all-inclusive depending on the vehicle and itinerary. International rental car agencies operate at the airport for self-driving itineraries. Petrol is cheaper than in Europe but more expensive than in the United States.

Marrakech-Menara International Airport is 5 kilometres from the city centre and offers extensive direct flights to European hubs (Paris, London, Madrid, Amsterdam are all daily and frequent), Casablanca for domestic connections, and seasonal direct service from major Gulf cities and select North American routes. Pre-arranged airport transfers from riads run 200–300 dirham; petit taxis from the airport rank charge 100–150 dirham (negotiate before departing). The new high-speed train, Al Boraq, connects Casablanca, Rabat, and Tangier but does not currently serve Marrakech; the conventional train from Casablanca to Marrakech takes 3 hours and costs 200–300 dirham in first class.

Cash dominates the transactional landscape but card acceptance has improved significantly in tourist-facing businesses. Most riads, mid-range and upscale restaurants, and tourist-oriented shops accept Visa and Mastercard; American Express is accepted only at upscale establishments. Smaller restaurants, taxis, and souk vendors are cash-only. ATMs are plentiful in Gueliz and around Jemaa el-Fnaa; withdrawal limits are generally 2,000–4,000 dirham per transaction with foreign-card fees of 25–35 dirham. The dirham is a closed currency — converting outside Morocco is difficult — so plan to convert at the airport or local exchange offices and use up remaining dirham before departure.

Connectivity is reliable and affordable. Maroc Telecom, Inwi, and Orange Maroc offer tourist SIM cards at the airport for 50–100 dirham including initial data; coverage in Marrakech and along major roads is excellent. WiFi is standard at riads, cafes, and most restaurants. Mobile data is the practical solution for navigation in the medina, where landmarks are easily lost behind walls.

Health and safety considerations for Marrakech are generally moderate. Tap water is potable in most modern hotels but bottled water is the safer default for visitors, particularly for street food and ice. Food hygiene at established restaurants is good; the open-air food stalls of Jemaa el-Fnaa have variable standards and are best chosen by selecting busy stalls with high turnover. Petty theft from tourists in the medina is uncommon but not unknown; standard travel precautions apply. Solo female travellers receive significant attention in the form of comments and persistent vendor approaches; this is rarely threatening but can be exhausting, and travelling with a partner or a small group meaningfully changes the experience.

Etiquette matters in ways that surprise some visitors. Modest dress — covered shoulders and knees minimum — is appreciated everywhere and required at religious sites. Public displays of affection beyond hand-holding are uncommon and uncomfortable for many Moroccans. Photographing people without explicit permission is genuinely intrusive and frequently leads to confrontation; the women in distinctive dress in Jemaa el-Fnaa charge for photographs as a matter of livelihood. The right hand is used for eating, greeting, and passing items; the left is considered unclean. Mosque entry is restricted to Muslims with the exception of the Hassan II Mosque in Casablanca and a handful of others.

Visa requirements for most Western nationalities are straightforward — visa-free entry for stays up to 90 days. The customs declaration is simple. Travel insurance covering medical evacuation is recommended given the limited capacity of the local hospital system for serious emergencies.
