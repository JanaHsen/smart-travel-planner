# Marrakech, Morocco — Overview

Marrakech sits on a plain at the foot of the Atlas Mountains in central Morocco, 580 metres above sea level. The city was founded in 1062 by the Almoravid dynasty as their imperial capital, and its old town — the medina — retains the same essential layout established in the 12th century: a maze of high-walled alleys, mosques, fondouks, and souks contained within nineteen kilometres of red-pink walls that give the city its nickname, the Red City. UNESCO inscribed the medina as a World Heritage site in 1985. The newer districts — the colonial-era Gueliz, the trendier Hivernage, and the suburban Palmeraie — sit outside the old walls and represent successive layers of 20th and 21st-century expansion.

The climate is semi-arid with strongly seasonal temperature swings. Summer, from June through September, is genuinely hot — daytime temperatures regularly exceed 38°C and frequently reach 45°C in July and August. The dry heat is more bearable than equivalent humid temperatures elsewhere but is still demanding for full-day sightseeing. Winter, from December through February, has pleasant daytime highs of 18–22°C, cool evenings dropping to 5–8°C, and infrequent rain. Spring (March through May) and autumn (October through November) are the most comfortable seasons and the most popular among visitors.

Best time to visit is shoulder season — March through May or October through November — when the weather is pleasant and the city is less oppressive than in peak summer. The Atlas Mountains an hour to the south remain snow-capped through April, providing dramatic backdrops on clear days. December and January are also good for visitors who tolerate cool evenings; prices are lower outside the New Year week and crowds significantly thinner. The week of Ramadan, which shifts each year through the Islamic calendar, brings substantial changes — many restaurants close during daylight hours, the rhythm of the city shifts to a late-evening peak after iftar, and some visitors find the experience richer while others find the daytime closures inconvenient.

Marrakech is one of the most visited cities in Africa and tourism is woven deeply into its economy. This produces a sometimes intense interaction with the visitor experience — persistent vendors, unsolicited "guides" in the medina, and pricing structures that frequently differ from what locals pay. Most of this is benign and easily managed with patience and friendly firmness; visitors prepared for the volume and persistence of tourist-targeted commerce generally have far better experiences than those who arrive expecting Western retail norms. Genuine warmth and hospitality are equally characteristic of the city — invitations to mint tea, casual conversations in the souks, and the generosity of riad hosts are routine.

Costs are moderate by global standards but have risen significantly with the city's tourism boom. Daily costs of $50–80 cover comfortable mid-range accommodation in a riad, three full meals, transport, and a couple of activities. Budget travellers can manage on $25–40 per day; luxury options are extensive and run from $200 to several thousand dollars per night for the most prestigious palace hotels. The tipping economy is significant — a few dirham for assistance with bags, restaurant rounding, and small gestures of thanks — and meaningfully expected.

The city is divided into distinct districts. The medina contains the famous Jemaa el-Fnaa square, the souks, the major historic monuments (the Koutoubia Mosque, the Bahia Palace, the Saadian Tombs), and the bulk of the riad accommodation. Gueliz, the Ville Nouvelle built during the French Protectorate, is laid out on a grid with wider streets, modern shops, and most of the city's contemporary art galleries and cafes. Hivernage, between the medina and Gueliz, holds many of the larger international hotels. The Palmeraie, north of the city centre, is a low-density palm grove with luxury resorts, golf courses, and large private villas.

Distances within the medina are short but navigation is genuinely difficult on a first visit. The alleys are unsigned, frequently dead-end, and twist in ways that make landmarks disappear behind walls within a few turns. Modern smartphone maps work imperfectly because GPS signals are weak between the high walls and many of the smallest passages are not in mapping databases. First-time visitors often hire a local guide for an initial half-day orientation; thereafter, getting genuinely lost in the medina becomes part of the experience rather than a problem to solve.
