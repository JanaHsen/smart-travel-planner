# Marrakech, Morocco — Things to Do

Jemaa el-Fnaa, the central square of the medina, is the gravitational centre of Marrakech and the natural starting point for any visit. The square transforms through the day — morning brings orange juice carts and snake charmers; afternoon adds storytellers, henna artists, and water sellers in elaborate traditional dress; evening ushers in dozens of food stalls serving grilled meats, snail soup, and mint tea, surrounded by musicians and small acrobatic troupes. The square has been listed by UNESCO as part of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity for the storytelling and oral performance traditions that continue daily. Watching the evening transformation from a rooftop cafe surrounding the square is one of the city's iconic experiences.

The souks of the medina spread north from Jemaa el-Fnaa in a vast covered market organized loosely by trade — leather goods, copper and brass work, spices, textiles, carpets, jewelry, and lanterns each have their own zones. Bargaining is universal and expected; opening prices typically run two to four times what the seller will ultimately accept. The carpet souks in particular involve elaborate hospitality rituals — mint tea, multiple unfurlings, extended conversation — that visitors should engage with on their own time terms or politely decline at the start. The dyers' and tanners' souks offer striking visual experiences; the tanneries in particular are pungent and best visited with a sprig of mint at the nose, which guides routinely provide.

The Bahia Palace, built in the late 19th century for a Grand Vizier, contains some of the finest examples of Moroccan craftsmanship — elaborately carved cedar ceilings, intricate plasterwork, geometric tile mosaics, and a series of courtyards organized around fountains. The palace is partially open as a museum and gives a sense of how Moroccan elite domestic architecture functioned. The El Badi Palace nearby is the picturesque ruined shell of a 16th-century Saadian palace; its sunken gardens and storks nesting on the high walls produce some of the city's most photogenic scenes. The Saadian Tombs, sealed for centuries and rediscovered only in 1917, contain elaborately decorated burial chambers of the dynasty.

The Majorelle Garden in Gueliz was created over forty years by the French painter Jacques Majorelle and later restored by Yves Saint Laurent. The garden's signature electric blue villa, the Berber Museum within the grounds, and the elaborate plantings of bamboo, cacti, and bougainvillea make it one of the most photographed spots in the city. The associated Yves Saint Laurent Museum sits next door and houses a permanent collection of the designer's work alongside rotating exhibitions. Combined entry tickets are available; advance booking online is strongly recommended as on-site queues frequently exceed an hour.

The Koutoubia Mosque, with its 77-metre minaret visible from across the city, is the largest mosque in Marrakech. Non-Muslims may not enter the prayer hall but the gardens surrounding the mosque are open to all and offer the best vantage point for photographing the minaret, particularly at sunset.

Hammam visits — Moroccan steam-bath rituals — are an essential cultural experience for many visitors. Public hammams (the local neighbourhood baths used by Moroccans) are inexpensive but spartan; tourist-oriented hammams in the riads and dedicated spas charge €25–80 for a full ritual including black soap exfoliation, kessa scrubbing, and rhassoul clay treatment. Several riads operate hammams that visitors can book without staying at the property; experienced visitors generally recommend booking at established spas rather than the busiest tourist hammams in the central medina.

Cooking classes are widely available and represent some of the best value cultural experiences in the city. Most operate from riads in the medina and include a market shopping trip, instruction in traditional tagines and salads, and sharing the resulting meal. Half-day classes run €30–60 per person; full-day immersions with multiple dishes are €60–100. The schools associated with established riads (La Maison Arabe, Café Clock, Amal) maintain consistently high quality.

Day trips into the surrounding region expand the experience considerably. The Atlas Mountains rise rapidly south of Marrakech; day trips to the village of Imlil at the base of Mount Toubkal include a guided walk through Berber villages, lunch at a traditional family home, and return by late afternoon. The Ourika Valley is a similar half-day to full-day option with seven cascading waterfalls. The Ouzoud Falls, 150 kilometres northeast, requires a longer day but is the country's most impressive waterfall. The coastal town of Essaouira, 175 kilometres west, is a 2.5-hour drive and offers a striking change of atmosphere — windswept Atlantic coast, Portuguese-built ramparts, and a more relaxed pace.

Camel and quad trips into the Agafay Desert, the rocky stone desert closer to Marrakech than the famous Sahara dunes, run as half-day, full-day, and overnight luxury camp experiences. The Agafay is genuinely scenic but not the classic golden-dune Sahara experience; visitors specifically seeking that landscape should plan for the longer 9–10 hour drive (each way) to Merzouga in the southeast or fly to Ouarzazate and continue from there.

Multi-day trips combining Marrakech with the Sahara, the Todra and Dades gorges, and the Berber villages along the Anti-Atlas typically run 3–5 days by minivan or 4x4 with a driver and overnight stops at desert camps and traditional kasbahs. These trips have become highly commoditized and quality varies enormously; researching reputable operators carefully is worth the time investment.
