# Portugal — Practical Information

Accommodation in Portugal offers good value by Western European standards across all categories, though prices have risen substantially with the tourism boom of the past decade. Hostel dorm beds run €20–40 per night in the major cities; private rooms in hostels and budget guesthouses €50–110. Mid-range hotels and small boutique properties in central Lisbon and Porto cost €100–220 per night for a double. Boutique hotels and well-positioned design properties run €200–450. The high-end tier — Four Seasons Ritz Lisbon, the Bairro Alto Hotel, the Six Senses Douro Valley, the Vila Joya in the Algarve — runs €400–1,800+ per night. The famous quinta accommodations in the Douro Valley, in restored historic estates with vineyard views, run €200–700 per night. Algarve resort pricing varies dramatically by season — beach resort rooms that cost €100–180 in shoulder season run €300–500+ in peak July and August. Short-term rental availability has been impacted by recent regulations in central Lisbon and Porto; legitimate licensed rentals remain widely available but the supply has tightened.

Food costs are reasonable. A casual lunch — soup, sandwich, coffee, or the prato do dia (daily plate) — runs €8–18 per person. The standard menú or pratos do dia at neighbourhood restaurants at lunch costs €10–18 for two courses with bread, water, and coffee. Dinner at casual restaurants runs €18–35 per person with wine; mid-range restaurants charge €30–60 per person. The famous seafood specialties at Lisbon and Algarve coastal restaurants — grilled sardines, cataplana, percebes (gooseneck barnacles), seasonal shellfish — can range from €15 for casual sardines through €40–80 per person for elaborate seafood meals. Several Michelin-starred restaurants charge €120–280 per person for tasting menus; Portugal currently has more Michelin stars per capita than most European countries. Tipping is not expected at the levels of North America; rounding up the bill or leaving 5–10% for excellent service is the local convention. Coffee culture is foundational — a bica (espresso) at a stand-up cafe counter runs €0.80–1.20; the same coffee at a sit-down table runs €1.50–2.50.

Public transport in Lisbon and Porto is excellent and the standard mode for visitors. Lisbon's metro network covers the city efficiently, with single tickets at €1.65 and the 24-hour pass at €6.80; the Viva Viagem rechargeable card simplifies multiple transit modes. The famous trams (Tram 28 in particular) operate on the same fare structure but require special tactical planning given tourist demand — boarding at the route ends rather than the central stops, and travelling outside peak hours, produces meaningfully better experiences. The Cascais and Sintra commuter trains from Lisbon run frequently and inexpensively (€2.30 to Cascais, €2.30 to Sintra). Porto's metro network is more limited but covers the major destinations including the airport.

Long-distance travel between cities is straightforward by train (Comboios de Portugal). The Alfa Pendular high-speed train connects Lisbon and Porto in 2 hours 45 minutes (€25–45 second class, €40–70 first class). The Intercity trains serve the broader network including Coimbra, Faro, Évora, and major regional centres. The Spanish high-speed rail connections from Madrid run via Badajoz to Évora in roughly 8 hours total; direct Lisbon-Madrid sleeper services have been suspended since 2020 but are periodically discussed for relaunch. Long-distance buses (Rede Expressos, Eva, Flixbus) cover the same routes at lower prices and longer journey times.

Renting a car becomes useful for the Alentejo, the Douro Valley, and the Algarve interior. Daily rental rates run €25–80 in shoulder season and €40–120 in peak summer. Petrol is among the most expensive in Europe at €1.70–2.00 per litre. The Algarve A22 toll road and the major motorways nationally use electronic tolling that requires either pre-arranged transponder rental from the rental agency or post-hoc payment through a complicated visitor system; clarifying the toll arrangement at the rental agency is essential. Driving in central Lisbon and Porto is genuinely difficult given the narrow streets, steep hills, and parking scarcity; rental cars are best collected on departure from these cities rather than during the urban portion of trips.

Lisbon Humberto Delgado Airport (LIS) sits within the city itself, 7 kilometres from the centre, and is connected to the centre by metro (Red Line, €1.65, 25 minutes), the Aerobus (€4.50), or taxi/Uber (€10–20). Porto's Francisco Sá Carneiro Airport (OPO) connects to the city centre by metro (Line E, €2.85, 30 minutes) or taxi/Uber (€20–30). Faro Airport (FAO) serves the Algarve with extensive seasonal direct flights from European hubs. Direct intercontinental flights are concentrated at Lisbon, with substantial direct service from major North American hubs (New York, Boston, Miami, Toronto, Newark) and from Brazil (the largest single international market for Lisbon flights given the Portuguese-Brazilian connection).

Cards are universally accepted at hotels, restaurants, museums, and most shops; Portugal is essentially a contactless-payment economy in the major cities. Visa, Mastercard, Apple Pay, and Google Pay work everywhere; American Express acceptance is more limited. Smaller traditional restaurants, bakeries, and rural businesses may be cash-preferred or cash-only. ATMs (Multibanco machines) are plentiful and charge no domestic fee for Portuguese-bank cardholders; foreign card fees of €3–6 per withdrawal apply at non-bank ATMs but are absent at the main bank ATMs of Caixa Geral de Depósitos, Millennium BCP, Santander Totta, and Novo Banco. The euro is the currency.

Connectivity is excellent throughout the country. EU roaming agreements mean European Union visitors use their domestic plans without surcharge. Non-EU visitors can buy Portuguese tourist SIMs from Vodafone Portugal, MEO, or NOS at the airport or in city electronics shops; multi-week packages with substantial data run €15–30. Free WiFi is universal at hotels, restaurants, cafes, and tourist information offices.

Safety considerations for Portugal are generally favourable — the country consistently ranks among the safer European destinations. The principal risk for visitors is petty theft and pickpocketing in central Lisbon (particularly Tram 28, the area around Rossio Square, and tourist-dense neighbourhoods) and in central Porto. Standard precautions — no valuables in back pockets, no phones on outdoor restaurant tables, awareness in crowded areas — substantially reduce the risk. Violent crime against visitors is uncommon. The hashish offers from street vendors in central Lisbon are widely reported to be fake products; engagement is not advised regardless.

Cultural and scheduling conventions follow general Mediterranean Europe patterns with some Portuguese specifics. Lunch is generally eaten between 12:30 and 2:30 p.m.; dinner is usually after 7:30 p.m., with restaurants frequently not opening for dinner before 7 p.m. The afternoon shop closure (siesta-equivalent) is less universal than in Spain but many small shops still close from 1 to 3 p.m. Sunday closures of small shops are common; the major chains and tourist-area establishments operate Sundays.

Visa requirements for most Western nationalities are simple — Schengen Area entry for stays up to 90 days within any 180-day period for visa-exempt travellers. Portugal enforces standard Schengen rules. The country's "Golden Visa" residence-by-investment program has been substantially restructured in 2023–2024; visitors should not confuse the popular tourism with separate residency arrangements. ETIAS authorization will become required for visa-exempt visitors during the rolling 2025–2026 implementation; checking current requirements before booking is the practical approach.

Health considerations are generally minor. Tap water is potable across the country; bottled water is unnecessary except by personal preference. Standard travel insurance covers the rare medical needs adequately; the Portuguese public health system provides emergency care to visitors with reciprocal arrangements for EU citizens. The summer sun is intense particularly in the Alentejo and Algarve; serious sun protection matters even on cloudy days. Sea conditions on the Atlantic coast can be dangerous; the bandeira system at supervised beaches (green/yellow/red flags) should be heeded.
