# Portugal — Things to Do

Lisbon rewards 3–5 days of dedicated exploration and is the natural starting point for most visits. The historic neighbourhoods of Alfama and Mouraria — the dense medieval districts that survived the 1755 earthquake — climb the hill above the river through narrow cobbled streets, with the Castle of São Jorge crowning the hilltop and offering the best panoramic views over the city. The Belém district to the west holds the Jerónimos Monastery (one of the country's most important Manueline-style buildings), the Belém Tower, the Monument to the Discoveries, and the famous Pastéis de Belém pastry shop where the original 19th-century recipe for the iconic custard tart is still made. The Bairro Alto and Chiado districts in the city centre concentrate the dining scene, the surviving traditional cafes, and the contemporary nightlife. The vintage Tram 28 runs a circuit through the central districts and is itself a tourist experience, though the route is genuinely useful as transport.

Porto, Portugal's second city, has emerged as nearly equally important to international visitors and rewards 2–3 days. The historic Ribeira district along the Douro River is a UNESCO World Heritage site, with its layered architecture climbing the hill from the riverfront. The famous Dom Luís I Bridge, designed by a student of Eiffel, crosses the Douro to Vila Nova de Gaia, where the major port wine houses (Taylor's, Graham's, Sandeman, Croft, Ferreira, Cálem, and others) operate cellar-door visits with tastings. The Livraria Lello, a Neo-Gothic bookstore that influenced the descriptions of Hogwarts in the Harry Potter novels, has become one of the most photographed bookshops in the world. Porto's restaurant scene has matured significantly with several Michelin-starred restaurants (The Yeatman, Antiqvvm) and a strong tier of mid-range establishments.

The Douro Valley, an hour inland from Porto, is one of the world's oldest demarcated wine regions and produces the country's most famous wines — port and increasingly fine still wines made from indigenous Portuguese grape varieties. The valley landscape of terraced vineyards climbing dramatically from the river is among the most striking wine landscapes anywhere. Visits range from day trips by train, road, or boat from Porto through multi-night stays at the historic quintas (wine estates) that increasingly operate as boutique hotels. The harvest season in September and early October offers visitor participation in traditional foot-treading at several estates; the autumn colours of the surrounding hillsides through November add to the landscape impact. The famous Régua and Pinhão areas concentrate the densest cluster of visitor-facing wineries.

Sintra, 30 minutes by train from central Lisbon, was the summer escape of the Portuguese royalty and aristocracy and contains an extraordinary concentration of palaces and gardens within a forested mountain landscape. The Pena Palace, an exuberant 19th-century Romantic-era construction in shockingly bright yellow and red, is the area's most photographed building. The Quinta da Regaleira's elaborate gardens contain the famous Initiation Well — a 27-metre inverted spiral tower that descends into a network of underground tunnels, designed for esoteric ritual purposes by a wealthy mystic in the early 20th century. The Moorish Castle ruins on the highest peak offer the best panoramic views. Sintra is genuinely overcrowded in peak season and on weekends; arriving at the first morning train and visiting Pena Palace at opening is the standard tactical advice. Combining Sintra with the coastal town of Cascais and the dramatic cliffs of Cabo da Roca (continental Europe's westernmost point) makes a long day trip from Lisbon.

The Algarve coast is the country's beach tourism centre and contains an extraordinary variety of coastal scenery within a 150-kilometre stretch. The famous golden cliffs and grottoes of the central Algarve — Praia da Marinha, Benagil Cave, the Algar Seco caves at Carvoeiro — are reachable from the resort towns of Lagos, Albufeira, and Carvoeiro and represent the region's iconic landscape. The eastern Algarve around Tavira retains a more authentic Portuguese atmosphere with traditional fishing villages and the Ria Formosa lagoon system protecting unique coastal ecosystems. The western Algarve from Sagres onward faces the open Atlantic with consistent surf and a more wild atmosphere; this is the country's surf capital and home to several internationally-recognized surf schools. Boat tours from the central Algarve resorts to the coastal grottoes are widely available; some require kayak access for the famous Benagil Cave's interior beach.

Surfing is increasingly central to Portugal's tourism identity and the variety of breaks suits all levels. The central coast around Peniche and Ericeira (the latter declared a World Surfing Reserve in 2011) offers consistent intermediate-friendly waves a 90-minute drive from Lisbon. Nazaré, slightly further north, is famous for the world's largest documented surf waves at the Praia do Norte break, where giant winter swells produce 25-metre faces ridden by professional big-wave specialists; the spectacle is best observed from the cliff lighthouse during November–February swell events. The Algarve coast (particularly around Sagres) and the western Alentejo coast offer extensive learning options through dedicated surf camps. Surf school instruction runs €40–80 for group lessons; multi-day surf camps with accommodation and instruction typically run €400–900 per week.

Évora and the Alentejo region offer a slower-paced alternative to the coastal and major-city tourism. Évora's old town is a UNESCO World Heritage site centred on a remarkably preserved Roman temple, the macabre Capela dos Ossos (a chapel decorated with the bones of 5,000 monks), and a Gothic cathedral. The Alentejo wine country surrounding Évora produces excellent reds from the indigenous Trincadeira and Alicante Bouschet grapes; the wine routes through the rolling cork-oak landscape make ideal driving itineraries. The walled hilltop town of Monsaraz overlooking the Alqueva reservoir offers some of the country's most striking sunset views. The cork industry — Portugal produces roughly half of the world's cork — provides a distinctive landscape of harvested oaks across the region.

The Atlantic island archipelagos offer their own dedicated experiences. Madeira, a volcanic island 1,000 kilometres southwest of mainland Portugal, has a year-round mild climate, dramatic mountain landscapes including the 1,818-metre Pico Ruivo, the famous network of levada irrigation channels providing extensive hiking trails, and the cliffside capital of Funchal with its excellent restaurant scene. The Azores, a chain of nine volcanic islands 1,500 kilometres west of mainland Portugal, offer dramatic crater lakes, hot springs, whale and dolphin watching among the world's most reliable, and a slower-paced atmosphere distinctly different from the tourism-saturated mainland destinations. São Miguel is the largest and most visited island; Pico, Faial, and Flores offer increasingly remote-island atmospheres for visitors with longer itineraries.

Food and wine experiences pervade Portuguese travel. Pastel de nata (custard tart) tasting tours through Lisbon, port wine cellar visits in Porto and Vila Nova de Gaia, traditional fado performances in Lisbon and Coimbra, fresh seafood meals in coastal towns, and the elaborate slow-cooked dishes of the Alentejo (porco preto, açorda, arroz de pato) are all easily integrated into general itineraries. Cooking classes are widely available in the major cities at €60–150 per person and are particularly rewarding given the depth and distinctiveness of Portuguese cuisine.
