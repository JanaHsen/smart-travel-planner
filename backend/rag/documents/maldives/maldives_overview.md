# Maldives — Overview

The Maldives is an archipelago of approximately 1,200 small coral islands organized into 26 natural atolls, stretching 870 kilometres north-to-south across the equator in the Indian Ocean. Roughly 200 of the islands are inhabited by the local population of around 540,000 people, and approximately 175 islands operate as private resorts hosting tourists. The total land area is just 298 square kilometres — among the smallest of any country — and the average elevation is 1.5 metres above sea level, making the Maldives one of the lowest-lying nations on earth and acutely vulnerable to sea-level rise.

The country is overwhelmingly defined by its natural environment: white-sand beaches, turquoise lagoons enclosed by coral reefs, and a marine biodiversity that includes whale sharks, manta rays, multiple shark species, and over 1,000 species of fish. The coral reefs themselves represent one of the largest and most accessible reef systems in the world, with diving and snorkeling that consistently rank among the global top tier. The capital Malé, on a single densely-populated island, contains the country's only international airport on the adjacent Hulhulé island, and is the gateway through which essentially all visitors arrive.

Tourism in the Maldives is structured around an unusual model: with rare exceptions, each resort occupies a complete private island, with the entire island's infrastructure dedicated to guests. The resorts vary enormously in price point — from $250 to $5,000+ per night — but share the structural pattern of being isolated from local population, requiring boat or seaplane transfer from the airport, and operating as self-contained worlds with their own restaurants, dive centres, spa facilities, and entertainment. A separate guesthouse industry, opened to foreign tourists only in 2009, allows visitors to stay on inhabited local islands at substantially lower prices ($60–200 per night) with a more authentic exposure to Maldivian daily life.

The climate is tropical with two seasons defined by monsoon patterns. The dry season, from November through April, sees less rain, calmer seas, lower humidity, and excellent visibility for diving and snorkeling — this is the peak tourist season with the highest prices. The wet season, from May through October, produces variable conditions with frequent but brief afternoon downpours, slightly cooler ocean temperatures, and rougher seas; prices drop substantially, particularly during May, June, and October. Within the wet season the visibility and conditions for diving are still good in much of the archipelago, particularly the southern atolls.

Best time to visit depends on priorities. For optimal weather and conditions, December through March is the prime window; this is also peak season with prices roughly 30–80% above shoulder rates. Late April and November offer good weather at slightly lower prices. For whale shark and manta ray encounters, May through November coincides with peak feeding seasons in specific atolls (South Ari Atoll for whale sharks; Hanifaru Bay in Baa Atoll for mantas). The shoulder months of late April and early November frequently offer the best balance of weather, marine life activity, and pricing.

Costs vary enormously based on accommodation choice. Resort packages including overwater bungalows, full meal plans, and seaplane transfers commonly run $1,000–3,000 per night for a couple at premium properties; entry-level resorts start around $400–600 per night including transfers and meals. Local-island guesthouses with full board run $80–250 per night for two people, with separate costs for activities. The country has minimal duty-free pricing benefits and most goods are imported, so casual purchases are typically more expensive than expected.

The Maldives is a Sunni Muslim country and Islam is the state religion; conversion or proselytizing is illegal, and alcohol and pork are not legally available outside of resort islands and dedicated tourist boats. On local islands and in Malé, modest dress is required (covered shoulders and knees for both genders), and the limited public beaches operate as "bikini beaches" specifically designated for tourist use. These rules do not apply on the private resort islands, where visitors can wear and consume whatever they wish.

Language is Dhivehi, but English is widely spoken at all resorts, dive centres, and tourist-facing businesses; communication with local-island residents in Malé and the capital region is straightforward in English, with somewhat more variability in remote atolls.
