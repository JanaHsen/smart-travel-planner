# Chiang Mai, Thailand — Overview

Chiang Mai sits in a fertile valley in northern Thailand, surrounded by the southernmost foothills of the Himalayan range. Founded in 1296 as the capital of the Lanna Kingdom, the city retains a distinctly different character from Bangkok: slower, cooler, more visibly Buddhist, and shaped by centuries of cross-border trade with what are now Myanmar, Laos, and southern China. The historic centre is a roughly 1.5-kilometre square moat surrounding the original walled city, still studded with ancient brick temples and now ringed by guesthouses, cafes, and small restaurants.

The climate has three distinct seasons that significantly affect the visitor experience. The cool season, from November through February, is the most popular and comfortable period, with daytime highs around 28°C, cool evenings that occasionally require a light jacket, and almost no rain. The hot season, from March through May, sees temperatures regularly above 35°C and frequently above 40°C in April; this period also coincides with widespread agricultural burning across northern Thailand and neighbouring countries, producing severe air pollution that can persist for weeks. The rainy season, from June through October, brings daily afternoon thunderstorms, lush green landscapes, and substantially fewer tourists; mornings are typically clear and the heat is more bearable than in the hot season.

Chiang Mai is one of the cheapest major destinations in Asia for foreign visitors. Daily costs of $25–40 cover comfortable mid-range accommodation, three full meals, local transport, and one or two paid activities. Budget travellers can manage on $15–20 per day; luxury options exist but rarely exceed what mid-range would cost in Bangkok or Singapore. This price level, combined with a large established expatriate community and reliable internet, has made the city a major hub for digital nomads, particularly in the Nimmanhaemin neighbourhood west of the old city.

The cultural and religious landscape is dominated by Theravada Buddhism, with over 300 active temples within the city limits. Wat Phra Singh, Wat Chedi Luang, and Wat Phra That Doi Suthep on the mountain overlooking the city are the most significant. Many temples conduct daily monk chats, where novice monks practice English by answering visitor questions about Buddhism and monastic life. Saffron-robed monks collecting morning alms are a routine sight in residential neighbourhoods.

The best time to visit is November through February for weather, but specific events shift the calculation. The Yi Peng and Loy Krathong lantern festivals in November draw enormous crowds and accommodation prices triple for the festival week — book six months in advance or avoid those specific dates. The Songkran water festival in mid-April is famous and chaotic but coincides with the worst air quality of the year. June and September fall in the rainy season but offer the best combination of low prices, thin crowds, and clean air.

Getting to Chiang Mai is straightforward. The international airport handles direct flights from most major Asian hubs and seasonal connections to Europe; an internal flight from Bangkok takes 75 minutes and frequently costs less than the overnight train. The overnight sleeper train from Bangkok takes 12–14 hours and is itself a memorable experience for travellers with time. Long-distance buses are cheap but slow.

The city is compact enough that the old town can be crossed on foot in 20 minutes. Songthaews — red shared pickup trucks — function as informal taxis throughout the city for 30–60 baht per ride. Renting a scooter is common among visitors comfortable with the traffic; international driving permits are required and increasingly enforced. Grab, the regional ride-hailing app, works reliably and removes most language barriers around transport.
