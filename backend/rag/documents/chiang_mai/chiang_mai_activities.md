# Chiang Mai, Thailand — Things to Do

The temple circuit of the old city is the obvious starting point and can be covered on foot in a single morning. Wat Phra Singh holds the most revered Buddha image in the north and features classic Lanna architecture; Wat Chedi Luang's enormous ruined chedi, partially collapsed in a 16th-century earthquake, is the most photogenic; Wat Chiang Man is the oldest temple in the city, dating to its founding. Doi Suthep, the mountain temple visible from anywhere in town, requires a half-day trip including the climb up 309 steps flanked by naga serpents; the panoramic view of the valley justifies the effort, particularly at sunset.

Cooking classes are perhaps the city's most popular tourist activity, with dozens of schools offering half-day and full-day courses. Most include a market visit to learn ingredients, hands-on preparation of four to six classic Thai dishes, and a printed recipe book to take home. Quality varies; established schools like Asia Scenic and Thai Farm are consistently well-reviewed and run small group sizes. Costs typically run 1,000–1,500 baht per person.

Elephant sanctuaries are heavily marketed and the ethical landscape is genuinely complicated. Camps that offer riding or shows should be avoided — the training process is brutal regardless of how the operation presents itself to visitors. Sanctuaries that allow only feeding, walking alongside, and bathing the animals represent the more responsible end of the industry, though even within this category practices vary. Elephant Nature Park, the longest-established rescue operation in the region, is the most consistently recommended; visits book out two to three weeks ahead in high season.

The surrounding countryside offers excellent trekking, particularly between November and February. Multi-day treks typically combine hiking through hill tribe villages, an overnight homestay, river rafting, and elephant interaction; one-day options are widely available for less ambitious itineraries. The Doi Inthanon National Park, surrounding Thailand's highest peak, is a half-day trip from the city and includes waterfalls, royal twin pagodas, and short forest walks. Mae Wang and Mae Taeng are two further-out trekking areas with denser jungle and fewer crowds.

Markets are central to the daily rhythm of the city. The Sunday Walking Street, running through the old city from late afternoon, is the largest and most touristed; the Saturday version on Wualai Road has a more local feel. The Warorot Market is a daily wholesale and retail market in the old Chinese quarter, excellent for spices, textiles, and dried foods. The Night Bazaar east of the moat operates every evening and is the most touristed but also the easiest to navigate for first-time visitors.

Outdoor activities expand considerably for the adventurous. The Sticky Waterfalls, an hour north of the city, allow visitors to walk barefoot up a series of mineral-coated cascades that grip the feet rather than slip. Rock climbing at Crazy Horse Buttress, 45 minutes east, offers over 150 routes for all skill levels. Whitewater rafting, ziplining, and ATV tours are all available within an hour of the city.

The Nimmanhaemin neighbourhood, west of the old city, is the modern centre of cafe culture, third-wave coffee, craft beer, and design boutiques. A full day can be spent wandering its grid of small streets and side alleys, working through specialty roasters and small galleries. The university quarter directly south is similar in feel but lower-priced and frequented mostly by Thai students.

Day trips beyond the immediate area include Chiang Rai, three hours north, for the famous White Temple (Wat Rong Khun) and the Black House (Baan Dam) — both contemporary works rather than traditional sites, but visually striking. Pai, three to four hours northwest, is a small town in the mountains popular with backpackers for its laid-back atmosphere and waterfalls; the winding road there is notorious for motion sickness.
