# Chiang Mai, Thailand — Practical Information

Accommodation prices in Chiang Mai are exceptionally reasonable across all categories. Hostel dorm beds run 200–400 baht per night and the city has some of the highest-quality budget accommodation in Asia. Mid-range guesthouses and small boutique hotels with air-conditioning, hot water, and pool access cost 800–1,800 baht per night for a double room. Mid-tier hotels in the Nimman or old city areas range from 1,800 to 4,000 baht. Luxury options include several restored Lanna-style resorts in the 6,000–15,000 baht range, and the high-end Four Seasons in the surrounding hills runs from 25,000 baht upward.

Food costs are remarkably low. A bowl of khao soi, the regional noodle curry, runs 50–80 baht at a street stall and 120–180 baht in a tourist-oriented restaurant. A full meal with rice, two dishes, and a drink at a local establishment can be had for under 150 baht. Mid-range restaurants serving international food charge 200–400 baht per main course. Coffee at the abundant specialty cafes runs 70–120 baht. Daily food budgets of 300–500 baht are entirely realistic for visitors willing to eat where locals eat; even doubling that figure produces a comfortable, varied diet.

Transport within the city is dominated by songthaews — red shared pickup trucks that function like buses without fixed routes. Flag one down, state the destination, and the driver will agree to a price (typically 30–60 baht per person within the central area) or signal that the route is not on their direction. Tuk-tuks are more expensive at 80–150 baht for similar distances. Grab, the regional ride-hailing app, works reliably and is often cheaper than tuk-tuks while removing the language barrier and price negotiation. Scooter rental at 200–300 baht per day is common, but Thailand's traffic environment is challenging and visitors without prior motorcycle experience should think twice; recent enforcement has made an international driving permit and helmet requirements increasingly standard.

Long-distance options are extensive. The international airport offers direct flights to Bangkok (75 minutes, 800–2,500 baht), Phuket, Krabi, and other domestic hubs, plus regional flights to Singapore, Kuala Lumpur, Hong Kong, and several Chinese cities. The overnight sleeper train from Bangkok costs 800–1,400 baht for a sleeper berth and takes 12–14 hours; second-class air-conditioned sleepers are comfortable. Buses to Bangkok run 600–900 baht and take 10–11 hours.

Cash is widely used but card acceptance has improved significantly. Most hotels, mid-range restaurants, and tourist-oriented shops accept Visa and Mastercard. Small restaurants, street food, and most markets are cash-only. ATMs are plentiful but charge a 220-baht foreign card fee per transaction; withdraw larger amounts to minimize the cumulative cost. Mobile payment via PromptPay is universal among Thais but generally unavailable to foreign visitors without a Thai bank account.

Connectivity is excellent and cheap. A tourist SIM with 15–30 days of data costs 300–700 baht at the airport or any 7-Eleven; coverage is reliable across the city and most surrounding areas. Free WiFi is standard at cafes, hotels, and many restaurants. The expatriate-driven cafe culture means it is easy to find air-conditioned workspaces with reliable internet for the price of a coffee.

Weather-related health concerns deserve specific attention. The burning season from late February through April produces air quality readings that regularly exceed safe thresholds; visitors with respiratory conditions should reconsider visiting during this period or stock up on N95 masks.
